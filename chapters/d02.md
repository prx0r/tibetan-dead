# Chapter II

## You're Dead Again. Please Stop Running Away from the Buddhas.

*Based principally on Karma Lingpa's Bardo Thödol, the Great Liberation Through Hearing, with reference to the complete Gyurme Dorje translation, the Dawa-Samdup translation, Karma Lingpa's Root Verses, and later Tibetan bardo commentaries. All dialogue and comedy are original.*

## 1\. You are alive\!

Congratulations\!

Against the overwhelming odds of beginningless saṃsāra, you've managed to acquire another human body.

Your last existence was something of a catastrophe.

You cultivated cruelty, died, missed the clear light of liberation, experienced an appallingly unpleasant hell rebirth, and eventually returned to the human realm.

But that's ancient history.

Literally.

You have absolutely no idea it happened.

You are currently three years old, standing in your parents' kitchen, holding a spoon.

You feel an overwhelming desire to throw it.

Why?

Who knows?

The spoon is here. The floor is there. You possess agency. Something must be done.

You launch it across the room.

Your mother looks exhausted.

And thus begins another magnificent human life.

This one goes rather better.

You develop a decent moral character. You acquire friends. You occasionally help people. You don't deliberately make anyone's life miserable.

In your thirties, you discover Buddhism.

You're fascinated.

No permanent self? Interesting.

The world is dependently arisen? Intriguing.

All conditioned phenomena are impermanent?

Absolutely. Couldn't agree more.

You buy several books.

You watch a lecture on Dzogchen.

You become extremely confident explaining emptiness to people who have not asked.

You briefly attempt meditation.

Unfortunately, after approximately eleven minutes, you discover that sitting quietly involves being alone with your own mind.

Absolutely unacceptable.

You return to researching Buddhist philosophy.

You accumulate a wonderfully sophisticated intellectual understanding of the doctrine.

You've even learned to pronounce *dharmatā*\.

But there's one minor problem.

**You understand the theory of nonattachment and are still personally attached to absolutely fucking everything.**

Your house.

Your possessions.

Your relationships.

Your body.

The particular way your morning tea tastes.

Your identity as someone who understands Buddhism.

Most disturbingly, you've developed a very strong attachment to being the sort of person who is not attached.

And then, at sixty-four, you're making breakfast.

You feel an unusual pressure in your chest.

You sit down.

Your arm goes numb.

And suddenly-

## 2\. You are dead.

Oh.

Right.

This again.

Now, according to the Tibetan tantric account, your ordinary experience doesn't simply vanish from an immortal soul like someone turning off a television.

Nor does the tradition identify clinical cardiac arrest with the completion of every aspect of its subtle death process.

Instead, it describes a progressive withdrawal of the functions supporting embodied experience.

Your physical strength fails.

Your senses lose their ordinary objects.

The breath ceases.

And the mental activity that depended on the body's grosser functioning begins to dissolve.

Here we need to revisit the sequence established in Chapter I, because the distinction matters enormously to what happens next.

The traditional sequence describes four coarse dissolutions, followed by increasingly subtle phases.

Earth into water.

Water into fire.

Fire into wind.

Wind into consciousness.

These are the four elemental categories of the Tibetan contemplative physiology, not the chemical elements of modern physics.

After these come the white appearance, red increase, black near-attainment, and the ground luminosity or clear light. In Patrul Rinpoche's account, these correspond to the dissolution of progressively subtler dualistic mental processes. \[[Brief Introduction to the Bardos](https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/patrul-rinpoche/bardo-introduction?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Liberation Upon Hearing](https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/minling-terchen/liberation-upon-hearing?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

It's a rather extraordinary theory.

The things which ordinarily constitute your sense of being a separate individual depend upon conditions.

As those conditions disappear, ordinary subject-object cognition is said to become increasingly subtle.

The conceptual machinery of *me, here, observing that, over there* falls away.

White luminosity.

Red luminosity.

Darkness.

Then-

**Clear light.**

And here you encounter your first opportunity.

The mind's fundamental nature is said to become manifest.

Not a physical light.

Not a colourful object floating in front of an invisible eyeball.

Not an enormous glowing Buddha standing in the corner of a hospital ward.

The tradition means the empty, luminous nature of awareness itself.

In the deepest Dzogchen framing, its emptiness and cognizance are inseparable.

Emptiness means it possesses no independent, self-existing essence.

Luminosity means that awareness is not a mere blank nothingness.

But you haven't developed stable recognition of this.

You have spent thirty years thinking about awareness without learning how to recognize its nature when conceptual structures dissolve.

The clear light dawns.

Your habitual ignorance remains.

And, once again, you fail to recognize it.

**Congratulations. You have missed the most important appointment of your entire life for the second time.**

Still, Tibetan Buddhism has excellent news.

The opportunity for liberation is not necessarily over.

In fact, depending on your preparation and circumstances, there may be further opportunities throughout the intermediate states.

Your death is about to become extraordinarily elaborate.

## 3\. The six bardos: Buddhism's deeply inconvenient understanding of your existence

Before we begin meeting deities, we must understand where the hell we actually are.

A *bardo* is an intermediate state.

But there's a philosophical trick hidden in that innocent definition.

**Everything you ordinarily call your life is already an intermediate state.**

You are between birth and death.

Between the emergence of one experience and its dissolution.

Between changing configurations of body and mind.

The Tibetan teachings describe six bardos, three associated with living experience and three particularly associated with death and rebirth. Karma Lingpa's actual *Root Verses on the Six Bardos* lays these out. \[[Root Verses on the Six Bardos](https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/karma-lingpa/root-verses-six-bardos?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

THE SIX BARDOS

| 1\. Life | The interval between birth and dying. The training ground. |
|--------|--------|
| 2\. Dream | The dream state, in which experience seems real until recognized. |
| 3\. Meditation | States of meditative absorption in which awareness is trained. |
| 4\. Dying | The dissolution of embodied functions and emergence of ground luminosity. |
| 5\. Dharmatā | The luminous display of reality's nature, including the peaceful and wrathful appearances. |
| 6\. Becoming | Karmically conditioned movement towards a new existence. |

Do you see the extraordinary implication?

The first three are not things you must die to experience.

**You have been rehearsing death your entire life.**

Every time you dream, you experience the appearance of a convincing world without the ordinary waking sensory environment.

You might dream that you're being chased by a tiger.

You run.

Your heart races.

You experience genuine fear.

Yet the tiger is not an independently existing animal in your bedroom.

The experience is dream-generated.

If you recognize the dream as a dream, the relationship between you and the tiger can fundamentally change.

You may remain lucid.

You may cease running.

You may understand that the appearance has no independent reality of the kind you originally attributed to it.

This is why dream yoga matters in Tibetan Buddhism.

It isn't merely learning to fly around lucid dreams because you've decided that ordinary aviation is insufficiently entertaining.

It is learning to recognize an appearance without being captured by it.

Now imagine a similar challenge at death.

An overwhelming experience arises.

You identify it as an independently existing object.

You react to it.

That reaction generates further conditioning.

And the cycle continues.

The great insight is that recognition might interrupt this mechanism.

But be precise: the Tibetan tradition does not thereby prove that waking reality is literally a private dream, or that other people are imaginary.

Phenomena are *empty of inherent existence*, not necessarily nonexistent.

Your mother, the spoon and the consequences of throwing the spoon are all conventionally real.

Emptiness is not a licence to assault the household with kitchen equipment.

Now, you've failed to recognize the clear light.

So we proceed to the fifth bardo.

The *chönyi bardo*\.

The bardo of dharmatā.

And what happens here is one of the most astonishing things in the history of religious literature.

Because the nature of awareness is about to appear in the form of an entire assembly of enlightened deities.

And you, being a complete novice in this department, are about to react with considerable alarm.

## 4\. Forty-two Buddhas have arrived. You are already frightened.

At first there is luminosity.

Then colour.

Then sound.

The entire experiential field becomes extraordinarily vivid.

These aren't the familiar colours of your living-room furniture.

According to the *Bardo Thödol*, the visions are radiant, overwhelming and accompanied by tremendous sounds, often compared to thunder.

An ordinary mind is accustomed to manageable sensory objects.

A cup.

A chair.

A tree.

Something over there that you can examine from the reassuring safety of over here.

But now there is no ordinary body supplying that stable perspective.

Experience becomes immense.

And something astonishing begins to emerge.

**A mandala.**

Not a decorative diagram someone purchased from a spiritually themed online shop.

A mandala in the tantric sense: an ordered manifestation of enlightened reality, structured through directions, colours, families, qualities and relationships.

At its centre are the Buddha families, traditionally associated with distinct forms of awakened wisdom.

This is where the Tibetan theory gets wonderfully sophisticated.

The teachings do not merely say that enlightenment involves eliminating the five principal disturbing emotions.

They also explain the transformation of these afflictions into wisdom.

Ignorance into the wisdom of the expanse of reality.

Anger into mirror-like wisdom.

Pride into the wisdom of equality.

Attachment into discriminating wisdom.

Jealousy into all-accomplishing wisdom.

The relationship isn't that you simply think a sufficiently positive thought and your anger magically becomes enlightenment.

It is that an affliction is a distorted way of apprehending reality.

When ignorance is undone, the same cognitive potential can function without its habitual distortion.

**Enlightenment isn't the destruction of your capacity to experience. It's the end of experiencing everything through compulsive misrecognition.**

Now watch what happens.

### The first day: Vairocana

An immense white Buddha appears upon a lion throne.

This is Vairocana, the central Buddha of the mandala.

From his heart radiates an unbearably brilliant blue light.

It represents the wisdom of the dharmadhātu: the expanse or fundamental nature of phenomena.

Alongside it appears another light.

A soft, dull white glow associated with the god realm.

Now you're given an extraordinary choice.

The brilliant light represents liberating wisdom.

The dull light represents an avenue of conditioned existence.

And which one do you prefer?

The dim one, obviously.

The blue luminosity is absolutely terrifying.

The white light is considerably more comfortable.

It's like being offered complete liberation from existential suffering and saying\:

*Do you have anything with softer lighting?*

But why would wisdom be terrifying?

Because the wisdom threatens the ignorant structure through which you understand yourself.

The ordinary ego relies upon distinctions.

I am here.

The world is there.

I possess this.

I desire that.

I must defend myself against something else.

Now the wisdom of the dharmadhātu represents a realization that undermines the inherent reality of those divisions.

To someone habituated to rigid self-reference, this can be experienced as profoundly destabilizing.

The problem is not that the wisdom intends to hurt you.

**The problem is that you interpret the collapse of ignorance as a threat to yourself.**

The text repeatedly instructs the deceased not to flee the brilliant light and not to become attracted to the dull one.

But you are absolutely convinced that the blue light is dangerous.

So you retreat.

First opportunity: wasted.

### The second day: Akṣobhya

Right.

Perhaps we should try something different.

Now Akshobhya appears, identified in this textual sequence with Vajrasattva.

From his heart comes a dazzling white light: mirror-like wisdom.

Alongside it is a dull smoky light associated with the hell realms.

Mirror-like wisdom means awareness capable of reflecting appearances without being distorted by anger, resistance or aversion.

Imagine a perfectly clear mirror.

Someone places a beautiful flower in front of it.

The mirror reflects the flower.

Someone places a hideous object in front of it.

The mirror reflects that too.

The mirror does not become personally offended.

It doesn't begin drafting an angry letter to the ugly object.

It simply reflects.

Your mind, however, has spent sixty-four years making everything personal.

Someone insults you: outrage.

Someone praises you: delight.

Someone disagrees: a direct assault upon the constitutional integrity of your entire personality.

And now there is a wisdom that reveals experience without that compulsive emotional distortion.

An extraordinary possibility.

Unfortunately, you see the overwhelming brightness and panic.

You turn toward the smoky light.

**You've just chosen the spiritual equivalent of a polluted industrial estate over perfect wisdom because the industrial estate looked less intimidating.**

The ancient text explicitly associates this danger with the karmic conditioning of anger. \[[The Tibetan Book of the Dead/Book 1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Tibetan_Book_of_the_Dead/Book_1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

### The third day: Ratnasambhava

Now Ratnasambhava appears.

Golden yellow.

Jewel in hand.

The wisdom of equality radiates from his heart.

This is not the proposition that all things are identical.

It is the realization that the ultimate nature of phenomena is not divided according to the ego's hierarchy of importance.

You have spent your life thinking\:

*I am particularly significant.*

*My accomplishments are particularly significant.*

*My preferences are particularly significant.*

And now wisdom reveals the absence of that inherent hierarchy.

There is an equality in emptiness which doesn't erase conventional differences.

A Buddha and an untrained being do not have identical capacities.

But neither exists as an intrinsically self-established entity.

This is a direct challenge to pride.

Alongside the brilliant yellow wisdom-light comes the dull human-realm light.

Ah\!

Humans\!

Wonderful\!

You understand humans.

You've been one.

You know how to buy groceries and complain about traffic.

Much better than whatever this enormous golden Buddha is proposing.

And once again, you are tempted toward a familiar samsaric existence.

### The fourth day: Amitābha

Now things become rather beautiful.

A red Buddha appears in the west.

Amitabha\.

He holds a lotus.

From his heart emerges the dazzling red light of discriminating wisdom.

This wisdom knows the distinct characteristics of phenomena without being enslaved by craving.

Here's an important philosophical distinction.

Buddhism doesn't teach that awakening destroys the ability to distinguish beauty from ugliness or pleasure from pain.

It does not render an awakened person cognitively incapable of appreciating a flower.

Instead, it removes the grasping which transforms appreciation into bondage.

You can perceive something beautiful without demanding that it belong to you.

You can encounter something unpleasant without constructing an entire personal identity around hating it.

That's discriminating wisdom.

Your ordinary attachment has a different strategy\:

*That thing is beautiful.*

*Therefore I must possess it.*

*Therefore I must preserve it.*

*Therefore I will become miserable when it changes.*

What an astonishingly stupid business model.

Alongside Amitābha's red luminosity appears the dull red light associated with the hungry-ghost realm.

The realm of compulsive wanting.

You've spent your life saying\:

*I just need one more thing.*

And now the karmic logic of craving is visible in its most extreme form.

A being endlessly reaching for satisfaction and incapable of achieving it.

Again, the question is whether you recognize the wisdom or follow the familiar habit. \[[The Tibetan Book of the Dead/Book 1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Tibetan_Book_of_the_Dead/Book_1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

### The fifth day: Amoghasiddhi

A green Buddha emerges in the north.

Amoghasiddhi\.

The associated wisdom is all-accomplishing activity.

Here jealousy and competitiveness are transformed into unhindered enlightened action.

Normally, you see someone else's achievement and experience it as evidence of your own inadequacy.

Someone publishes a brilliant book.

You immediately feel an urgent need to explain why their interpretation is slightly deficient.

Someone becomes successful.

You experience their happiness as a personal insult.

Someone achieves liberation.

You ask whether their credentials are internationally recognized.

But enlightened activity doesn't operate through the desperate need to become superior.

It acts appropriately and compassionately without that self-centred competition.

Beside the brilliant green wisdom-light appears the dull green light of the asura realm: the realm associated with jealousy, rivalry and warfare.

Once again, the familiar distortion offers its own destination.

And once again, you hesitate before the wisdom. \[[The Tibetan Book of the Dead/Book 1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Tibetan_Book_of_the_Dead/Book_1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

### The sixth day: The entire fucking mandala

At this point the Tibetan death manual has apparently concluded that subtlety is not working.

The individual Buddha families have appeared.

Their wisdoms have been presented.

You've been given repeated opportunities for recognition.

And you've treated the whole thing like an exceptionally alarming museum exhibition.

So now, in the text's sixth-day vision, the peaceful mandala appears together.

The five Buddha families.

Their consorts.

The bodhisattvas.

The female bodhisattvas.

The six Buddhas associated with the six realms.

The gatekeepers.

And the primordial pair Samantabhadra and Samantabhadrī.

The full assembly of forty-two peaceful deities is present.

The count can be understood as two primordial figures, ten figures representing five Buddha couples, sixteen bodhisattva figures, six Buddhas of the realms, and eight gatekeeper figures.

These are not forty-two independent creators.

They form a single mandalic representation of enlightened qualities.

The different figures disclose distinct aspects of wisdom, compassion, enlightened activity and the purification of ordinary experience.

Brilliant wisdom radiances appear together.

So do the dull lights of the six conditioned realms.

One thing needs particular precision: the classic sixth-day sequence emphasizes the *four wisdom lights united*, explaining that the green light of all-accomplishing wisdom does not manifest in the same way at that stage. It isn't merely an uncomplicated five-colour light show. \[[The Tibetan Book of the Dead/Book 1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Tibetan_Book_of_the_Dead/Book_1?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [The Tibetan Book of the Dead/Addenda](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Tibetan_Book_of_the_Dead/Addenda?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

## 5\. Why are these deities appearing inside your mind?

Now, before we proceed to the blood-drinking monsters, we must answer a serious question.

What exactly are these beings?

Are they independent supernatural entities?

Are they hallucinations?

Are they symbols?

Are they representations of psychological states?

The most precise answer is that their status depends on which level of Buddhist teaching and interpretation we are discussing.

In the Karma Lingpa cycle, the peaceful and wrathful manifestations are presented as appearances of awareness's own nature, within a tantric cosmology in which deity, wisdom, body and mandala are profoundly interconnected.

The text tells the deceased to recognize the appearances as their own display, rather than as external enemies.

But **that is not quite the same as reducing them to random hallucinations generated by a dying brain.**

A modern psychological interpretation might treat the visions as personifications of mental processes.

A traditional tantric interpretation takes their status as enlightened appearances much more seriously.

And an academic historical interpretation asks what these figures meant within the specific Nyingma ritual world in which the teachings developed.

Those aren't interchangeable explanations.

Consider the distinction between an ordinary dream and an awakened appearance.

In an ordinary dream, the mind constructs appearances under the influence of ignorance and habit.

The dreamer mistakes those appearances for independently existing things.

In the bardo of dharmatā, however, the tradition describes an opportunity for the nature of awareness to reveal itself through luminous forms.

The decisive issue is recognition.

Does the experience become another object of fear, attraction and identification?

Or is its empty, luminous character recognized?

This is where Dzogchen's language of *rigpa*, awakened awareness, becomes important.

Rigpa is not simply the fact that you're having an experience.

You can be exquisitely aware that you're terrified and still be completely ignorant of the nature of awareness.

The relevant recognition is nondual.

It does not set up a separate observer possessing an independently existing object called enlightenment.

**The appearance and its knowing are recognized without the ordinary error of treating subject and object as self-existing entities.**

Karma Lingpa's *Root Verses* explicitly direct the practitioner to recognize whatever appears as the display of awareness and not to fear the peaceful and wrathful deities. \[[Root Verses on the Six Bardos](https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/karma-lingpa/root-verses-six-bardos?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

In other words, the text isn't primarily teaching you to memorize a catalogue of supernatural beings.

It's teaching you not to be deceived by the apparent separateness of experience.

And now comes the really funny part.

Because you've failed to recognize a single one of the peaceful manifestations.

So the same enlightened reality is about to return.

**This time carrying skulls.**

## 6\. The seventh day: the knowledge-holders throw a party

Before the terrifying part, an important transitional appearance takes place.

On the seventh day, the text describes an assembly of *vidyādharas* - knowledge-holders - accompanied by ḍākinīs.

These are presented as dancing, radiant figures associated with tantric realization.

Five knowledge-holder couples appear in the traditional account.

The display is energetic, dynamic, colourful.

They're not merely sitting solemnly in meditation.

They dance.

The iconography is extraordinary.

Skull cups.

Curved knives.

Radiant ornaments.

Female tantric figures in union with male knowledge-holders.

And now you are beginning to suspect that the afterlife has turned into an extremely exclusive tantric nightclub.

But the imagery has doctrinal significance.

Tantric Buddhism does not necessarily identify awakened realization with withdrawal from all appearance and activity.

Liberating wisdom can be represented dynamically.

Dancing is an expression of movement without bondage.

The union of male and female deities often signifies the inseparability of wisdom and skilful means.

The skull cup evokes impermanence, the transformation of defilements, and the radical transcendence of ordinary conceptual boundaries.

These symbols belong to a ritual system, not an invitation to literalize every terrifying object.

One numerical detail: the seventh-day knowledge-holders are an additional assembly, not simply ten more members to add to the canonical hundred peaceful and wrathful deities.

The forty-two peaceful forms and fifty-eight wrathful forms are the celebrated hundred; the knowledge-holders occupy the transitional seventh-day vision in the sequence transmitted through the text. \[[The Tibetan Book of the Dead/Book 1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Tibetan_Book_of_the_Dead/Book_1?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [The Tibetan Book of the Dead/Addenda](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Tibetan_Book_of_the_Dead/Addenda?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

And you?

You still haven't recognized what's happening.

You see the dancing figures.

You hear the extraordinary sounds.

You experience the tremendous energy of the visionary display.

And you conclude\:

*This is getting a bit much.*

So you retreat again.

The peaceful deities have failed to secure your recognition.

The knowledge-holders have failed to secure your recognition.

And now the eighth day begins.

There is a sound like thunder.

Not a polite distant rumble.

An unimaginable eruption.

Oh, good.

This is Buddha-Heruka, the first great wrathful manifestation of the sequence.

The text identifies this manifestation with the awakened nature previously presented in peaceful form through Vairocana.

And here is the essential teaching\:

**The wrathful deity is not an enemy of enlightenment. It is an expression of enlightenment.**

The problem is entirely your interpretation.

You look at the horrifying appearance and think\:

*It wants to kill me.*

The tradition says\:

*Recognize its nature.*

You think\:

*It has six hands and is holding a human skull.*

The teaching says\:

*Recognize its nature.*

You think\:

*I would prefer to conduct this conversation from a considerable distance.*

But you have no stable physical body.

And within the traditional explanation, the terrifying apparition is inseparable from the very awareness through which it is experienced.

There is nowhere you can run that takes you outside the fundamental problem.

Now we must distinguish wrathful compassion from ordinary anger.

An angry person reacts through aversion and self-defence.

A wrathful Buddha, within tantric symbolism, expresses uncompromising activity directed toward the destruction of ignorance.

The wrathfulness is not a permission slip for human cruelty.

A practitioner becoming furious with their neighbours is not thereby manifesting an enlightened Heruka.

That would be an extremely convenient doctrine for unpleasant people.

The symbolism is much more demanding.

Imagine compassion that refuses to cooperate with delusion.

Compassion that does not indulge your comforting lies.

Compassion that cuts through the structures by which you continually manufacture suffering.

That is the ideal conveyed through wrathful enlightened imagery.

But it doesn't look particularly cuddly.

And you, naturally, are absolutely shitting yourself.

### Days nine through twelve: four more attempts

The next days unfold through the remaining four great Heruka families.

On the ninth day, Vajra-Heruka appears.

On the tenth, Ratna-Heruka.

On the eleventh, Padma-Heruka.

On the twelfth, Karma-Heruka.

These are wrathful counterparts to the Buddha-family structure already disclosed.

Blue, yellow, red and green qualities return through a ferocious iconographic vocabulary.

And the deceased is repeatedly instructed to recognize the appearances.

The logic is extremely important.

This isn't a game where you must defeat a sequence of increasingly difficult monsters.

The appearances are not separate obstacles which must be conquered before enlightenment can be awarded.

**The obstacle is your failure to recognize what appears.**

You see terror.

You establish it as something independently real.

You locate yourself as its potential victim.

Fear arises.

The fear generates a desperate impulse to escape.

And that impulse reinforces the original conviction that an independently real danger exists.

There's a self-amplifying structure here.

Fear appears.

Fear is believed.

Belief intensifies the appearance.

The intensified appearance generates more fear.

In the dream analogy, you're running from a tiger that gains strength every time you run.

But the fundamental alternative is not to become stronger than the tiger.

It's to recognize the nature of the dream.

### Day thirteen: things become considerably worse

Now the text describes further terrifying female manifestations, including eight wrathful goddesses and eight animal-headed figures in this stage of the sequence.

They carry implements associated with charnel grounds, death and tantric transformation.

A lion-headed figure.

A tiger-headed figure.

A wolf-headed figure.

Forms combining human and animal characteristics.

Your mind is now producing what appears to be the most aggressively experimental horror film ever conceived.

And the instruction remains unchanged.

Do not fear.

Recognize.

On the fourteenth day, further animal-headed yoginīs and guardians appear, completing the elaboration of the fifty-eight wrathful figures.

Their mandala includes the ten principal Heruka-couple figures, two groups of eight wrathful female figures, four wrathful gatekeepers and twenty-eight animal-headed yoginīs.

The detailed iconography differs across textual and artistic presentations, but the canonical division remains forty-two peaceful and fifty-eight wrathful.

And why all these extraordinarily frightening forms?

Because the tantric system insists that even the terrifying appearances ordinarily associated with defilement, death and dissolution need not be intrinsically opposed to wisdom.

**Nothing appearing in experience possesses the independent nature your fear attributes to it.**

That includes the terrifying things.

In the bardo of becoming, Tibetan texts describe a mind-made or mentally conditioned body, sometimes called a *yid lus*\.

The important claim isn't that a permanent spiritual soul has simply stepped out of a discarded meat suit.

Rather, a karmically conditioned consciousness-continuum experiences a body-like form without the ordinary physical organism.

This intermediate body is described as having functional senses, extraordinary mobility and an appearance influenced by both the previous life and the prospective next one.

It can move through ordinary obstructions.

It can travel rapidly under the influence of thought.

It may perceive other beings of similar karmic conditions.

It may perceive living relatives without being able to communicate with them.

The *Bardo Thödol* describes the deceased seeing family members, attempting to speak, receiving no reply and becoming distressed upon realizing the situation. \[[The Tibetan Book of the Dead/Book 2](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Tibetan_Book_of_the_Dead/Book_2?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

| Gods - devas | Extraordinary pleasure and power, but still impermanent. Even the gods die. |
|--------|--------|
| Demi-gods - asuras | Rivalry, competition, jealousy and conflict. |
| Humans | Mixed pleasure and suffering, but a particularly valuable opportunity for Dharma. |
| Animals | Vulnerability, fear, exploitation and impediments to reflective spiritual practice. |

## 14\. The final philosophical revelation: you were running from yourself

Attraction.

Aversion.

Fear.

Grasping.

Identification.

This is the mechanism the teachings are trying to interrupt.

You knew the names of the Buddha families.

You knew that the five poisons could be transformed into five wisdoms.

### What does liberation actually interrupt?

The answer is not the mere occurrence of appearances.

Nor is it simply fear.

Fear is one of the symptoms of a deeper structure.

The fundamental ignorance is the reification of experience: treating dependently arisen phenomena, including the apparent subject, as possessing independent essence.

From that misrecognition arise grasping, aversion and compulsive becoming.

A Buddhist master need not become incapable of feeling, perceiving or acting.

The liberating insight concerns the nature of those experiences and the cessation of the ignorance that binds them into saṃsāra.

And that is why the Tibetan Book of the Dead is not merely an elaborate map of the supernatural.

It is a teaching about the relationship between appearance, recognition and bondage.

Its extraordinary claim is that the same basic problem continues across death.

It further claims that the crisis of dying presents unusually powerful opportunities for liberation.

Science has not established postmortem bardos, karmic rebirth or the objective occurrence of the hundred deity visions. How quickly a thought becomes an identity.

How quickly an emotion becomes a world.

And how much suffering depends upon the assumption that these passing configurations are independently real and must be defended.

Perhaps there is no empirical evidence that a luminous mandala awaits you after death.

And in the Tibetan tradition, that is precisely the point.

You are already in a bardo.

You are already encountering appearances.

You are already practising the reactions you may carry into death.

You are already cultivating either recognition or confusion.

Which brings us to the most uncomfortable conclusion of all.

**You don't need to die to discover whether you spend your life running away from the nature of your own experience.**

You can find that out this morning.

And if the Tibetan theory is right, what you do about it may have consequences considerably beyond this particular lifetime.
