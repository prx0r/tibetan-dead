# Chapter III

## You Fed Some Geckos, Chanted at Them, and Now You're Dead.

*A wildly irreverent, philosophically rigorous exploration of merit, karma, mantras, Buddha-fields, devotion and the decidedly peculiar economics of enlightenment.*

*Our principal sources for this chapter are Śāntideva's Bodhicaryāvatāra, the Kāraṇḍavyūha Sūtra, the Sukhāvatīvyūha sūtras, Karma Lingpa's Bardo Thödol, and Tibetan aspiration prayers by Mipham Rinpoche, Jigme Lingpa and others. They belong to related but distinct Indian Mahāyāna and Tibetan traditions.*


## 1\. You are alive\!

Again.

Bloody hell.

You have now completed two spectacularly unsuccessful attempts at escaping saṃsāra.

In your first life, you were a vicious bastard and subsequently discovered that the hell realms were not particularly hospitable.

In your second, you were a reasonably decent human being who understood an impressive amount of Buddhist philosophy and absolutely none of it when you needed it.

You encountered the clear light of death.

Missed it.

Forty-two peaceful deities.

Missed them.

Fifty-eight wrathful deities.

Ran away screaming.

A karmic tribunal.

Complained about the administration.

Finally, propelled by habitual craving and aversion, you acquired another human birth.

And here you are.

A child.

A new body.

A new brain.

A fresh opportunity to discover that everything you love is impermanent.

But something is different.

This time, your life develops an unexpected characteristic.

**You actually give a shit about other beings.**

Not merely because being nice might make people like you.

Not because you want a spiritually respectable personality.

Not because you've discovered that generosity gets you a better reincarnation package.

You see suffering, and something in you wants it to stop.

An injured bird.

A hungry animal.

Someone crying.

You feel their distress.

And for the first time in this long, ridiculous story, you don't immediately turn everything back toward yourself.

At twenty-three, you're living somewhere warm.

There are two little house geckos who frequently emerge onto your table.

One appears to keep watch.

The other goes adventuring.

The lookout regards you with the intense suspicion normally reserved for exceptionally dangerous furniture.

The explorer investigates tiny insects around the table.

You become absurdly fond of them.

One day you place some clean water nearby and make the area safer for them.

The geckos interpret this as evidence that the enormous mammal is behaving unpredictably.

You whisper a Buddhist mantra.

The lookout freezes.

The explorer retreats.

You smile.

You've just performed what you believe to be a compassionate Buddhist act.

The geckos think you're fucking insane.

And now comes the genuinely important question.

**Could this possibly matter to what happens after any of you die?**

According to Tibetan Buddhism: yes.

Potentially, profoundly.

But not for the ridiculously simplistic reason people often imagine.

The gecko has not acquired a spiritual bank account.

You have not electronically transferred it three hundred enlightenment points.

And the Buddha is not standing in a celestial accounting office recording that a small reptile received an unusually attentive customer-service experience.

The theory is much stranger.


## 2\. What the fuck is merit?

Let's begin with a distinction that makes nearly everything else intelligible.

In Sanskrit, *karma* means action.

Within Buddhist ethics, the decisive factor is intention: *cetanā*\.

An action is karmically significant not merely because a physical event occurred, but because of the volition accompanying it.

The Tibetan word for merit, *bsod nams*, translates Sanskrit *puṇya*\.

Merit is the positive karmic potential associated with wholesome actions and mental states.

But please understand this.

**Merit is not a substance.**

It isn't some invisible golden liquid that accumulates in your skull.

It isn't a metaphysical cryptocurrency.

You cannot mine it by leaving a mantra playing on seventeen computers while you go out and behave like an absolute bastard.

It is better understood, within Buddhist causal theory, as the beneficial potential of ethically wholesome activity.

And that potential operates within a system of interdependent causes and conditions.

Imagine two identical-looking actions.

You feed a hungry animal because you are genuinely concerned for its suffering.

Or you feed the animal because someone attractive is watching, and you want them to think you're spiritually magnificent.

Physically, the animal receives the same food.

But mentally, you have enacted quite different intentions.

In the first case, compassion is operative.

In the second, self-promotion dominates.

The animal may benefit in both cases, but the karmic character of your intention differs.

The first action weakens the tendency to make everything about yourself.

The second may reinforce it.

And then a third possibility arises.

You feed the animal while wishing that this act may support the eventual awakening of every sentient being.

Now the action has acquired an explicitly Mahāyāna orientation.

The immediate act is small.

The scope of the intention is immense.

This is where merit becomes philosophically fascinating.

### You are not merely performing actions. You are becoming disposed to perform them.

Suppose someone insults you.

You respond with anger.

The next time someone insults you, anger arises more readily.

You have strengthened a habit.

Now suppose you deliberately restrain the reaction.

You acknowledge the hurt.

You consider that the other person is also subject to ignorance, insecurity and suffering.

You respond without cruelty.

The next time, that possibility is more available.

This is a psychological analogy for karmic conditioning, although classical Buddhist karma extends well beyond what psychology can presently demonstrate.

In Tibetan doctrinal systems, wholesome and unwholesome actions establish causal potentials within the continuity conventionally called a person.

Different philosophical schools explain the basis of that continuity differently. Yogācāra accounts can use the language of seeds within storehouse consciousness, while Madhyamaka commentators may reject treating such a consciousness as an ultimately existing repository.

The common point is that no permanent soul or metaphysical bank vault is required.

There is causal continuity.

And because the continuum is conditioned, its future is not fixed independently of present action.

That is why what you do now matters.

Not because the universe is handing out rewards for being polite.

But because **the character of intentional activity helps condition the possibilities that follow it**\.

Traditional Buddhism extends that causal relationship into future births. That extension is a religious and philosophical claim, not an experimentally established result.

### The six perfections: the actual curriculum

Your teacher eventually introduces you to the six *pāramitās*, the perfections of the bodhisattva.

Generosity.

Ethical discipline.

Patience.

Diligence.

Meditative concentration.

Wisdom.

And he explains that these aren't simply six ways of becoming a nicer person.

They're methods for systematically undermining the structures that maintain self-centred ignorance.

Generosity challenges possessiveness.

Ethics challenges the impulses through which you harm others.

Patience challenges reactive aversion.

Diligence challenges the inertia that keeps you from practising.

Concentration stabilizes attention.

Wisdom investigates the absence of inherent existence.

Do you see how beautifully they fit together?

Ordinary compassion is not abandoned.

It's developed into a systematic path.

And generosity is no longer merely an occasional act of social kindness.

It becomes a practice in loosening the grasping through which you organize your life.

You give something away.

And in doing so, you discover that the imagined solidity of *mine* can become less absolute.

This is already interesting.

But your teacher has something considerably more outrageous to tell you.

You can dedicate the merit of your actions.

To other beings.

Even beings who have no idea you're doing it.

Including, apparently, your two deeply suspicious geckos.


## 3\. Excuse me, can I transfer my karma to a lizard?

You ask your teacher a perfectly reasonable question.

*If everyone inherits the consequences of their own karma, how the fuck can I give merit to someone else?*

Excellent.

This is one of the real philosophical tensions in Buddhist theories of merit-transfer, not something to dismiss with mystical handwaving.

Classical Buddhism maintains that intentional actions have consequences for the continuum performing them.

Yet merit-dedication is widespread throughout Buddhist traditions, particularly Mahāyāna Buddhism.

How can both be true?

First, we must distinguish **dedication from the literal transfer of a substance**\.

The Sanskrit term *pariṇāmanā* means something like dedication, transformation or directing toward an end.

When you dedicate merit, you orient the wholesome potential of an action toward a particular aim.

You might dedicate it to the health of someone who is ill.

To the favourable rebirth of someone who has died.

To the welfare of an animal.

Or to the complete awakening of all sentient beings.

Your own intentions have thereby changed.

The action is situated within a larger pattern of compassion, aspiration and practice.

But how does that actually benefit another continuum?

Here Buddhist explanations differ.

In some traditions, beings who learn of or rejoice in another person's virtue generate their own wholesome karma through that rejoicing.

Other Mahāyāna and tantric explanations place greater emphasis upon dedication, interdependence, blessings, ritual efficacy and the responsiveness of sentient beings to awakened activity.

Scholars have identified genuine debates about whether merit is transferred, shared, transformed or made available as a condition for someone else's benefit.

Here's the philosophically precise answer.

**You cannot simply erase another being's karmic history by deciding to be generous.**

If that were possible, the Buddha could have ended the entire problem of saṃsāra by distributing infinite merit to everyone.

Roll credits.

Buddhism completed.

Unfortunately, Buddhist traditions generally hold that liberation involves the transformation of ignorance and its causes within each being's continuum.

The compassion and activity of Buddhas and bodhisattvas provide enormously powerful conditions for that transformation.

But a sentient being is not ordinarily made fully awakened by having someone else's realization mechanically inserted into its mind.

Now the gecko becomes important.

You cannot force it to attain enlightenment.

You cannot compel it to understand emptiness.

You cannot override the totality of its karmic conditions by offering water and reciting six syllables.

But you can attempt to establish beneficial conditions.

You can avoid harming it.

You can help relieve suffering.

You can cultivate compassion.

You can expose it, within the traditional religious understanding, to sounds and symbols regarded as beneficial.

And you can dedicate your virtue toward the conditions of its future awakening.

Your teacher explains\:

*You are cultivating the causes of benefit. You are not controlling another being's destiny.*

You look at the gecko.

The gecko looks at you.

You experience an extraordinary moment of interspecies spiritual solidarity.

The gecko walks away.

Frankly, it has other shit to do.


## 4\. Śāntideva enters the room and absolutely destroys your understanding of generosity

Your teacher gives you a book.

It's called the Bodhicaryāvatāra\.

Its author, Śāntideva, was an Indian Buddhist monk and philosopher traditionally dated to around the eighth century.

You expect something gentle.

Perhaps some encouraging reflections about being kind to people.

Instead, the book develops an ethical ideal so radical that your entire understanding of ownership begins to disintegrate.

Śāntideva asks the practitioner to dedicate their body, possessions and virtues to the welfare of other beings.

Not occasionally.

Not when convenient.

Not merely to those who have been particularly pleasant.

All beings.

Even enemies.

Even those who insult or harm you.

Even creatures you've never encountered.

Even beings suffering in realms whose existence you have no way of verifying.

The bodhisattva ideal aims at nothing less than the liberation of all sentient beings.

And crucially, Śāntideva's third chapter links this transformation to the arising of *bodhicitta*\: the determination to attain complete awakening for the benefit of all beings.

The tenth chapter then expands dedication into a vast series of aspirations for the relief of suffering throughout saṃsāra. 

Now you begin to understand why dedication is not merely an optional little prayer added to the end of meditation.

It fundamentally changes what you're trying to accomplish.

Suppose you perform a virtuous action because you want a comfortable future rebirth.

Fine.

Within the Buddhist framework, that's a wholesome worldly motivation.

But it remains organized around *your* future welfare.

Now suppose you perform that action and dedicate it toward the enlightenment of all beings.

The scope has changed.

You've taken a finite action and incorporated it into an aspiration that does not terminate with your own satisfaction.

**You are training yourself to stop treating your personal happiness as the final objective of existence.**

This is a much more profound transformation than simply becoming good at charity.

### Why doesn't giving merit away mean you lose it?

Your first question is wonderfully revealing.

*If I dedicate all my merit to other beings, haven't I just given away my chance of enlightenment?*

Your teacher stares at you.

You've spent twenty years studying Buddhist philosophy and have apparently concluded that compassion operates like withdrawing money from an ATM.

But merit is not a finite substance in this model.

Dedication is itself a wholesome intentional act.

Rejoicing in the welfare of others is wholesome.

Expanding one's motivation beyond personal reward is wholesome.

Consequently, the very act of dedicating merit is understood to strengthen the bodhisattva orientation.

This is not ordinary arithmetic.

You're not starting with a hundred units of merit and subtracting eighty-nine because you've donated them to seventeen insects and a gecko.

Instead, dedication changes the causal direction and significance of the virtuous activity.

And there's an even subtler teaching.

The highest form of dedication is united with the wisdom of emptiness.

You recognize that the giver, the recipient and the gift are all empty of inherent existence.

This doesn't mean there is no gift.

It means there is no ultimately self-existing giver who possesses an ultimately self-existing virtue and transfers it to an ultimately self-existing recipient.

The entire situation is dependently arisen.

You act compassionately without turning compassion into another object of egoic possession.

**You are no longer trying to become the proud owner of a magnificent collection of good deeds.**

Which is unfortunate, because you were considering having a small trophy made.


## 5\. Bodhicitta: the moment the entire direction of the journey changes

Now the geckos have become regular visitors.

You recognize the lookout immediately.

The other one has developed a remarkable capacity to appear whenever you begin eating.

One evening, you are watching them.

The smaller gecko is hunting a tiny insect.

You notice its extraordinary attentiveness.

Its nervous movements.

Its sensitivity to every little vibration.

And something occurs to you.

In the Buddhist interpretation, this creature is also a sentient being.

Not a failed human.

Not an inferior object created for your entertainment.

A living being experiencing its own form of existence, subject to vulnerability, hunger, danger and death.

Now extend that insight.

Perhaps this creature has existed in other forms.

Perhaps, according to the doctrine of beginningless rebirth, the relations between beings have changed countless times.

The Tibetan *lojong* and *lamrim* traditions frequently develop compassion by contemplating that all sentient beings have, across beginningless lives, been intimately related to us.

One common contemplation imagines that every being has at some time been one's mother.

The point is not to establish a literal genealogy of your current house gecko.

It is to break down the arbitrary distinction between the handful of beings whose suffering matters to you and the apparently infinite others whose suffering you habitually disregard.

And so you formulate an aspiration.

May this creature be free from suffering.

May it have the conditions for awakening.

May every sentient being have the conditions for awakening.

And may I attain the wisdom and compassion necessary to help them.

**That last step is bodhicitta.**

Not merely wishing people happiness.

Not merely feeling sad when something suffers.

But aspiring to Buddhahood precisely because complete awakening is understood to provide the fullest capacity to benefit beings.

Traditional Mahāyāna teaching distinguishes the bodhicitta of aspiration from the bodhicitta of engagement.

One determines to travel.

The other actually sets off.

You can sincerely wish to help all beings while still being a rather dreadful practitioner.

The next step is doing the work.

Developing ethics.

Developing patience.

Developing concentration.

Investigating emptiness.

Cultivating the capacities required to make compassion effective.

### The problem with merely feeling compassionate

You are watching your beloved geckos.

You think\:

*I love these little bastards.*

Wonderful.

But suppose you then deliberately create a dangerous environment for them, feed them unsuitable food, or interfere with them in a way that causes stress.

Your pleasant compassionate feelings do not cancel the harm.

A bodhisattva must develop wisdom about what actually benefits beings.

Compassion without discernment can become sentimentality.

Discernment without compassion can become cold intellectualism.

And tantric Buddhism will eventually describe an inseparability of *prajñā*, wisdom, and *upāya*, skilful means.

You need both.

You cannot simply throw goodwill at the universe and assume everyone has been helped.

Nor can you reduce sentient beings to research objects because you've become exceptionally clever.

The ideal is compassion informed by knowledge.

Which brings us to a rather extraordinary possibility.

Perhaps, according to the tradition, you can benefit a being even when it cannot comprehend your teachings.

Perhaps the connection itself can become a cause of something.

Now the mantra enters.


## 6\. Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ: six syllables, and apparently an absurd amount of metaphysical responsibility

You've learned a mantra.

ॐ मणि पद्मे हूँ

**Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ.**


It's the great six-syllable mantra associated with Avalokiteśvara - Chenrezig in Tibetan.

And you decide to chant it softly in the presence of your geckos.

The lookout is deeply unimpressed.

The explorer briefly examines a piece of furniture.

Your teacher tells you that this mantra can establish extraordinary beneficial connections with sentient beings.

You understandably ask\:

*How?*

Excellent question.

But first, the historical source.

The *Kāraṇḍavyūha Sūtra*, translated into English by Peter Alan Roberts as The Basket's Display, is one of the foundational Indian scriptures for the six-syllable mantra.

Its written emergence is generally placed around the fifth century CE, long before Karma Lingpa's fourteenth-century Tibetan treasure-text cycle.

It portrays Avalokiteśvara performing extraordinary compassionate activities across multiple realms and describes the immense benefits of remembering, reciting, possessing and transmitting the mantra.

It even narrates a bodhisattva's enormous quest to obtain the mantra from a Dharma teacher who has broken his monastic vows.

A wonderful little detail.

The most precious liberating formula in the story is not simply handed over by an impeccably respectable, beautifully credentialed monk.

The teaching exceeds the conventional prestige of its human bearer.

Now let's explain what makes the mantra significant.

### What do the syllables actually mean?

*Oṃ* is a sacred syllable with a long history in Indian religious traditions.

*Maṇi* means jewel.

*Padme* is related to lotus, but the famous English rendering about a jewel being inside a lotus is not a securely literal translation of the whole mantra.

There are grammatical and historical complications involving Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit and the interpretation of the compound as an invocation.

*Hūṃ* is another sacred syllable, particularly significant in tantric contexts.

In later Tibetan exegesis, the syllables acquire a wealth of symbolic associations.

The jewel can represent compassionate method.

The lotus can represent wisdom.

Their union signifies a path in which compassion and the realization of emptiness are inseparable.

The Dalai Lama has explained the mantra in terms of the transformation of ordinary body, speech and mind into awakened body, speech and mind through the inseparable cultivation of method and wisdom. 

And the six syllables can be associated with the purification of the six realms.

Now watch how perfectly this connects to our story.

In Chapter I, the six realms appeared as possible outcomes of karmic conditioning.

In Chapter II, their dull lights appeared as alternatives to the brilliant luminosities of wisdom.

Now the six-syllable mantra is employed as a practice that symbolically and ritually addresses the afflictions sustaining those realms.

The ordinary mind is dominated by ignorance, attachment, hatred, pride, jealousy and craving.

The mantra is associated with the compassion capable of counteracting these afflictions.

But here is the decisive distinction.

**The original Kāraṇḍavyūha describes extraordinary benefits without providing every later Tibetan syllable-by-syllable explanation.**

Those elaborate correspondences developed within subsequent traditions, including the *Maṇi Kambum*\.

They are meaningful traditional interpretations, but we should not falsely present each one as a word-for-word translation from the early Indian sūtra.

Now you're ready for the really outrageous story.


## 7\. Avalokiteśvara becomes a fucking bee

I'm not inventing this.

This is genuinely in the *Kāraṇḍavyūha Sūtra*\.

In one episode, Avalokiteśvara encounters an enormous population of insects living in a cesspit in Vārāṇasī.

These are not distinguished Buddhist scholars.

They are insects.

Living in filth.

They cannot attend a lecture.

They cannot learn Sanskrit grammar.

They cannot read a philosophical commentary.

They cannot examine the two truths through sustained Madhyamaka analysis.

So what does the great bodhisattva do?

**He becomes a bee.**

Absolutely magnificent.

An enlightened being with the cosmological capacities attributed to Avalokiteśvara surveys the situation and decides that the appropriate vehicle for compassionate activity is a small buzzing insect.

He approaches the creatures.

And his buzzing conveys homage to the Buddha, Dharma and Saṅgha.

The insects hear and remember the refuge formula.

The text then describes a profound transformation through which they attain rebirth in Sukhāvatī as bodhisattvas.

This is not a modern metaphor that I have invented.

It is an actual episode of the sūtra, at section 1.83 in the 84000 translation.

Now consider the implications.

The text is making a radically inclusive soteriological claim.

Liberating influences are not restricted to intellectual understanding.

The capacity to receive a beneficial condition might exist even in beings incapable of understanding the teaching conceptually.

And the enlightened bodhisattva can adapt the means of teaching to the condition of the recipient.

This is *upāya*, skilful means, taken to an almost absurdly literal extreme.

You cannot give the insects a discourse on dependent origination.

So the bodhisattva teaches in a form they can encounter.

**An enlightened being has become a buzzing insect because the insects are the beings who need help.**

The spiritual dignity of the recipient is not dependent upon its ability to understand a university lecture.

That is a genuinely beautiful principle.

But we must distinguish the episode's doctrinal claim from what we can independently know.

The sūtra depicts Avalokiteśvara's extraordinary salvific power.

It does not establish that an ordinary human chanting near a gecko will reproduce the same result.

You are not Avalokiteśvara.

Your gecko is not necessarily going to become a fully awakened bodhisattva by next Tuesday.

And the sūtra's story does not constitute experimental proof that sounds cause insects to enter Buddha-fields.

Nevertheless, its compassionate logic is clear.

There is no category of sentient being whose welfare is inherently irrelevant.

Not even the creatures in a cesspit.

Not even the ones crawling around your table.

And certainly not merely because they are incapable of understanding your religious vocabulary.

The gecko reappears.

You whisper the mantra.

It regards you suspiciously.

You're beginning to wonder whether you have acquired the correct transmission for speaking to reptiles.


## 8\. How could a mantra actually work?

Your teacher has become accustomed to your relentless questioning.

You approach him again.

*Right. Seriously. What is the mechanism?*

*How can sound possibly affect karma?*

*And why would looking at six syllables printed on a rock make any difference?*

Excellent.

Let's examine the traditional explanations without smuggling in fake quantum physics.

### First: ordinary psychological causation

When you repeatedly recite a mantra, you engage attention, memory, bodily rhythm, auditory perception and intention.

You are less likely, during that particular moment, to be wholly consumed by a stream of unrelated thoughts.

If you associate the mantra with compassion, its recitation can help bring compassionate intentions to mind.

Repeated practice may help make those responses more familiar.

These are intelligible psychological mechanisms.

They do not require the existence of rebirth.

But they also don't establish all the supernatural benefits attributed to the mantra.

### Second: karmic conditioning

Within Buddhist theory, an intentional act can leave a causal potential that ripens when supporting conditions arise.

A mantra may be associated with the Dharma even when the being hearing it cannot currently comprehend the teaching.

A future encounter with similar teachings may then be described as the ripening of a previous karmic connection.

This is often explained through metaphors of seeds.

The sound plants a seed.

Conditions later allow it to mature.

But these are metaphysical claims about the continuity of consciousness across lives.

They are not established descriptions of how sound waves rewrite postmortem destiny.

### Third: blessing and enlightened activity

Tibetan Buddhism also employs the concept of *adhiṣṭhāna*, often translated as blessing, inspiration or empowering influence.

Here the mantra isn't regarded as merely a useful sequence of phonemes chosen for their psychological effects.

It is understood to embody a connection with awakened speech and compassionate activity.

The lineage, the practitioner, the sacred formula and the awakened qualities it invokes are understood as causally related.

An accomplished practitioner is not merely remembering an abstract moral ideal.

They are participating in a ritual relationship with awakened reality.

Within the tradition, this may be considered efficacious even when the ordinary mind cannot explain how.

But here's the philosophical problem.

How could an enlightened being affect another being without simply overriding karma?

The answer usually involves conditions rather than absolute control.

A Buddha's activity may provide powerful conditions.

The recipient's karmic connections, receptivity and circumstances still matter.

The interaction is not ordinarily understood as a creator deity suspending all causality by whim.

The tradition does make stronger claims in certain scriptures and rituals; those should not be softened into mere psychology. But neither do they authorize an unrestricted claim that any ritual invariably liberates anyone.

### Fourth: dependent origination

Now we reach the point where people begin invoking quantum entanglement and everything goes to shit.

Dependent origination means that phenomena arise through causes and conditions.

That is a profound Buddhist philosophical principle.

But it does not imply that every desired effect follows from every imaginable cause.

If you chant a mantra near a gecko, dependent origination does not by itself establish that the animal will attain Buddhahood.

That requires an additional account of precisely how the relevant conditions are connected.

Different Tibetan traditions provide such accounts through karma, mantra, blessings, ritual consecration, enlightened activity and the nature of mind.

You may find these coherent within their religious metaphysics.

But **coherence is not the same as empirical verification**\.

And no respectable scientific account currently demonstrates that seeing or hearing a mantra produces liberation after death.

Research into meditation does support some psychological and health-related effects of certain practices, but these findings cannot verify rebirth or the extraordinary claims of tantric scripture.

Your teacher listens to all this.

You ask\:

*So should I keep chanting?*

He replies, in our entirely fictional dialogue\:

*Does the practice help you cultivate compassion and wisdom?*

You consider this.

Yes.

*Then perhaps start there.*

You return to your geckos.

The lookout has disappeared.

The explorer is attempting to climb onto your cup.

You whisper\:

*Oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ.*

And for once, you do not demand an immediate supernatural result.


## 9\. Liberation through seeing: Did Buddhism really invent enlightenment by looking at things?

Now we come to one of the most extraordinary features of Tibetan religious practice.

You visit a temple.

There are prayer wheels.

Mantras carved into stone.

Sacred images.

Small rolled texts carried as protective objects.

And someone explains that certain ritual objects are associated with liberation through seeing, hearing, wearing, touching or tasting.

You stare.

*Are you telling me I can become enlightened by looking at a fucking drawing?*

The answer is complicated.

And the complication is fascinating.

Tibetan sources describe a family of practices often grouped under the idea of liberation without extensive formal meditation.

The names include\:

- *Mthong grol* - liberation through seeing.
- *Thos grol* - liberation through hearing.
- *Btags grol* - liberation through wearing or attachment.
- *Myong grol* - liberation through tasting.
- *Reg grol* - liberation through touching.
- *Dran grol* - liberation through recollection.

The lists and classifications vary across traditions.

These are not all chapters of the *Bardo Thödol* itself.

The term *thos grol* is central to the title of Karma Lingpa's liberation-through-hearing instructions.

Other sensory-liberation methods are found across wider Tibetan tantric and Dzogchen literature.

And unlike the popular assumption that such objects belong only to unsophisticated folk religion, the tradition has developed sophisticated philosophical accounts of their efficacy.

### The amulet that supposedly does more than you do

Imagine a small sacred text.

It is copied.

Rolled up.

Placed inside a protective container.

Then worn on the body.

Certain Nyingma *terma* traditions claim that such objects can provide exceptionally powerful benefits, including liberation.

You are understandably confused.

*Hang on.*

*I've spent years meditating.*

*And this fellow is wearing a tiny book in his shirt and apparently getting the same result?*

This question turns out to have a genuine history.

In his 2025 study, James Gentry examines the philosophy of liberation-through-wearing amulets in Nyingma Heart Essence traditions.

He shows that the practice was not understood uniformly as a crude automatic effect.

Interpretations developed around copying, wearing, analysing, recognizing and practising the significance of sacred texts.

In particular, commentary associated with Guru Chöwang, the thirteenth-century treasure revealer, helped integrate intellectual examination with the practice of wearing sacred scriptures.

The relationship between the material text and liberating knowledge was itself a subject of reflection.

This is much more interesting than saying\:

*Magical necklace. Enlightenment acquired.*

The object can be understood as participating in several overlapping relationships.

It materially embodies sacred language.

It establishes a connection to a lineage.

It can serve as a focus of devotion.

Its use may be prescribed through a specific ritual transmission.

Its meaning may be contemplated.

And some traditions attribute to it a blessing not reducible to ordinary conscious comprehension.

**The sacred object is not simply information stored on paper. It is a physical participant in a religious practice.**

But we should be careful.

It doesn't follow that you can print an arbitrary collection of Tibetan characters, wear it to the supermarket and claim to have bypassed the path to liberation.

Traditional claims of extraordinary efficacy belong to particular texts, consecrations, ritual systems and interpretations.

And whether their strongest supernatural promises are objectively true remains unverified.

### Liberation through hearing: why the dead are read instructions

Now return to the *Bardo Thödol*\.

In Chapter II, you repeatedly failed to recognize the appearances of the bardo.

The ritual reader's task is to remind the deceased of their nature.

The teaching is spoken.

The deceased is addressed.

The appearances are identified.

The instructions are repeated.

The text makes exceptionally strong claims about the liberating power of hearing these teachings, even for beings with terrible karma.

The strongest traditional claim should not be hidden.

Some passages insist that hearing itself may be sufficient under extraordinary circumstances.

But the practical structure of the instructions also relies upon recognition, remembrance and karmic connection.

That is why training during life matters so much.

Imagine someone whispering a mathematical theorem into your ear.

If you've spent years studying mathematics, those words may immediately activate a complex structure of understanding.

If you've never learned arithmetic, the same sounds may mean almost nothing to you.

Now, Tibetan tantric claims exceed this analogy because they sometimes attribute transformative power to sensory contact independently of conceptual understanding.

But the analogy explains why recognition can depend upon prior habituation.

The teaching becomes familiar.

A sound brings a practice to mind.

A sacred image evokes an awakened quality.

A remembered instruction interrupts an automatic reaction.

And if the tradition's postmortem model is true, those habits might matter enormously during the bardo.

You therefore make a sensible resolution.

You're going to practise while alive.

Rather than gamble everything on a mysterious book being read aloud after you've lost the capacity to understand what the hell is happening.


## 10\. Why devotion is not necessarily intellectual surrender

You are now thirty-five.

You've cultivated compassion.

## 11\. Buddha-fields: Have the Buddhas built their own fucking universes?

You thought there were six realms.

Now someone tells you that there are innumerable Buddha-fields.

Excellent.

Apparently the cosmology has expanded again.

What exactly is a Buddha-field?

The Sanskrit term *buddhakṣetra* refers to a field or realm associated with a Buddha.

In Mahāyāna Buddhism, such realms are understood in connection with a Buddha's vows, merit, wisdom and enlightened activity.

Some descriptions present extraordinarily pure environments in which practice is favoured and defilements are absent or greatly diminished.

Others discuss impure realms, purification of perception, or the inseparability of Buddhahood and the nature of ordinary appearances.

The idea isn't always that the Buddha has purchased some celestial real estate and hired an architect.

Instead, Buddha-fields are profoundly connected with the perfection of the Buddha's activity.

In certain Mahāyāna accounts, a bodhisattva makes vast vows specifying the conditions of the world in which they will eventually teach.

As those vows are fulfilled through the accumulation of merit and wisdom, a Buddha-field is established.

Now, that raises a fascinating metaphysical possibility.

**Could a world be structured by the awakening of the consciousness associated with it?**

In ordinary saṃsāra, experience is conditioned by ignorance and karma.

In a Buddha-field, the environment is described as manifesting the fruit of enlightened vows and activity.

There is an apparent symmetry.

Ignorance conditions environments conducive to continued bondage.

Awakened compassion can establish environments conducive to liberation.

But be careful: this does not mean the Buddha-fields are simply private dream-worlds imagined by individual minds.

Traditional Mahāyāna Buddhism commonly treats them as genuine realms accessible to suitable beings, while Madhyamaka insists that all such phenomena remain empty of inherent existence.

Nor does the Buddhist doctrine follow automatically from modern theories of information or consciousness.

The comparison is philosophically interesting.

It is not scientific confirmation.

Now you discover that one of these Buddha-fields has a particularly reassuring feature.

It is extraordinarily difficult to make the usual catastrophic mistakes there.

You immediately become interested.

### Why would a Buddha make such a place?

Because, in the traditional account, Amitābha was previously a bodhisattva whose vows sought to establish the conditions for the liberation of other beings.

The larger Sukhāvatī tradition describes this process through the story of Dharmākara.

Dharmākara makes extraordinary vows about the realm he will establish upon Buddhahood.

His aspirations concern the welfare of beings who come into contact with his Buddha-field and the possibility of attaining awakening there.

Within the framework of these sūtras, the pure land results from the Buddha's vast vows and accumulated virtues.

The shorter *Sukhāvatīvyūha*, preserved in the Tibetan Kangyur as Toh 115, presents the field and the conditions for rebirth there. The longer *Array of Amitābha*, Toh 49, develops the background of the vows. 

And now you understand why Pure Land practice is so attractive.

You are not claiming to have already achieved perfect recognition.

You are acknowledging that you're still subject to ignorance.

You would like to continue the path somewhere exceptionally favourable.

**You want to stop trying to attain enlightenment while constantly being sabotaged by the ordinary conditions of saṃsāra.**

But here's the critical question.

Does birth in Sukhāvatī mean you have already attained final Buddhahood?

Not necessarily.

The point of rebirth there, in many Tibetan presentations, is that it offers exceptionally favourable conditions for completing the bodhisattva path.

The practitioner progresses toward awakening in an environment associated with the direct presence of Amitābha and the teachings.

This differs from imagining the pure land as eternal personal enjoyment.

And it differs from the ordinary god realms of saṃsāra, in which pleasure is still conditioned by karma and eventually comes to an end.

Sukhāvatī is not merely the deva realm with significantly better landscaping.

It is a realm whose defining purpose is the fulfilment of the path to enlightenment.


## 13\. Right. How do I actually get into Sukhāvatī?

The Buddha's enlightened activity also matters.

## 14\. Tuṣita: Another heaven? Oh, for fuck's sake.

Now you hear about a second extraordinary destination.

Tuṣita.

In Tibetan, *Ganden*\.

This is the celestial realm associated with Maitreya\.

You ask your teacher\:

*Is this another pure land?*

*Another heaven?*

*Are there different departments?*

And the answer requires a distinction that often disappears in popular explanations.

In traditional Buddhist cosmology, Tuṣita is the fourth of the six heavens of the desire realm.

That makes it part of saṃsāra.

Gods dwelling there, considered simply as devas enjoying the fruits of wholesome karma, remain subject to impermanence and rebirth.

But Tuṣita also has an extraordinary role as the present abode of Maitreya, the bodhisattva destined to become the next Buddha in our world.

Traditions distinguish his special dwelling or inner domain from the more ordinary celestial existence associated with Tuṣita.

The inner court, in influential accounts, is the environment where Maitreya teaches.

The distinction becomes especially important in traditions that explicitly interpret Maitreya's Tuṣita as a pure realm.

And it's not merely a modern attempt to make Buddhist cosmology tidier.

Scholar James B. Apple has studied the Geluk tradition's understanding of Maitreya's Tuṣita as a Buddha-field, including connections with Tsongkhapa and the monastery of Ganden.

So you've got two related but distinct meanings.

Ordinary Tuṣita as a deva heaven.

And Maitreya's special teaching environment, understood in certain traditions as a pure land.

You cannot simply collapse the two.

### Why would anyone want to go there?

Because Maitreya is traditionally regarded as the future Buddha.

He is expected to descend into the human world when the relevant conditions arise, attain complete Buddhahood and teach the Dharma.

Aspiring to encounter him means aspiring toward an exceptionally favourable relationship with the teachings.

In Geluk Buddhist traditions, Maitreya and Tuṣita have special devotional significance.

Now imagine the appeal.

### Sukhāvatī versus Tuṣita

Here is the essential difference.

Sukhāvatī is presented as Amitābha's pure Buddha-field, established through enlightened vows and uniquely favourable for liberation.

Tuṣita is also an established heaven within the ordinary Buddhist cosmological hierarchy, but Maitreya's special abode there is treated as a field of teaching and purification within influential devotional traditions.

A conventional rebirth as a Tuṣita god does not automatically imply liberation.

Nor should every aspiration toward Maitreya simply be treated as identical to rebirth in Sukhāvatī.

The goals, cosmological descriptions and associated practices differ.

But both can function as powerful devotional aspirations.

And both illustrate the same fundamental principle.

**Where and how you continue practising matters.**

If rebirth is real, the circumstances of the next existence could either obstruct or support progress toward awakening.

That is why Tibetan Buddhism is so concerned with the quality of the mind at death.

Not because the last thought is an omnipotent magic switch capable of erasing all previous karma.

But because death is described as a critical transition in which powerful karmic dispositions, habitual tendencies and aspirations may influence the subsequent course.

And you have now spent decades developing rather different dispositions from those that dominated your previous lives.

The third death is approaching.


## 15\. The scientific question: Could intention actually organize future experience?

Now we must confront the most interesting possibility without pretending that we have experimentally demonstrated Tibetan cosmology.

The central Buddhist account is that intentional activity can shape the continuity of experience.

The psychological component is familiar.

Habits alter subsequent behaviour.

Repeated attention changes what becomes salient.

Practices of compassion may change how people interpret and respond to suffering.

Learning establishes patterns that influence future perception and action.

These are legitimate subjects for psychology and neuroscience.

But the traditional karmic theory makes a considerably stronger assertion.

It proposes that intentional actions can condition future existence beyond the death of the current organism.

The Pure Land doctrine adds a further claim: devotional aspiration, virtue and the activity of awakened Buddhas can support birth in a radically different environment.

Neither proposition has been scientifically established.

And ordinary neuroscience does not provide a mechanism by which a moral intention causes a postmortem consciousness to enter a Buddha-field.

You cannot solve that explanatory gap by simply invoking quantum coherence, bioelectricity or nonlocal information.

Those concepts have specific scientific meanings.

Any serious proposed connection would need a model explaining how the relevant causal structure survives biological death, how it carries information, how it influences a new organism or environment, and what observations could distinguish it from competing explanations.

But there is a philosophical connection worth preserving.

**A person's future experience is not independent of the structure of their present activity.**

In ordinary life, this is obvious.

A person who repeatedly cultivates resentment may come to experience the world as hostile.

A person who cultivates generosity may become more disposed to notice opportunities for care.

A person who repeatedly rehearses a devotional image may find that image readily available at moments of stress.

There is a feedback loop between intention, attention, habit and experience.

The Tibetan theory extends that causal continuity across death.

That is the speculative leap.

And if it is true, the implications are extraordinary.

Your mental habits could matter even after the neural architecture through which they were ordinarily expressed has ceased functioning.

But that is precisely what requires explanation.

Not an excuse to declare that ancient Buddhists secretly discovered quantum neuroscience.

Now, back to the story.

Because your own death is approaching.

And this time, you have something that was missing from your previous lives.

Not perfect realization.

Not mastery of Dzogchen.

Not the ability to manipulate subtle winds through the central channel.

**A deeply familiar orientation toward compassion.**

And the aspiration that whatever happens next may become a cause of awakening.

## 20\. The final lesson: Merit does not buy liberation. It helps create the conditions for it.

Let's finish with the fundamental philosophical insight of this chapter.

You began this lifetime with a strange question.

Could feeding an animal, chanting a mantra or dedicating a good deed alter the course of rebirth?

The traditional answer is that such actions can establish beneficial causes and conditions.

But the deepest Mahāyāna answer goes further.

Compassion isn't merely a technique for acquiring a superior rebirth.

Merit isn't merely a reward for ethical activity.

Bodhicitta isn't merely an especially generous emotional state.

They are methods for changing the structure of your relationship with experience.

The ignorant mind repeatedly makes itself the centre.

*What do I want?*

*What threatens me?*

*What can I acquire?*

*What must I defend?*

The bodhisattva develops an alternative orientation.

*How can suffering be relieved?*

*What causes liberation?*

*How can I cultivate the capacities to help?*

*How can I act without treating myself and others as independently existing objects?*

And there is an extraordinary philosophical progression here.

First, you learn to avoid harming beings.

Then you learn to care for them.

Then you dedicate your virtue to their welfare.

Then you aspire to complete awakening for their sake.

Then you investigate the emptiness of the giver, the gift and the recipient.

**Compassion and wisdom begin to undermine the very self-centred ignorance that perpetuates saṃsāra.**

This is why the third lifetime represents such a decisive change.

You haven't simply improved your moral reputation.

You have begun to transform the orientation of consciousness itself.

And if Tibetan Buddhism's account of postmortem continuity is true, that transformation may be profoundly consequential beyond death.

But the final objective is still liberation.

Not endless accumulation of merit.

Not infinite good deeds performed by an eternal ego.

Not becoming a celestial philanthropist who possesses the largest collection of virtue in the known universe.

The aim is the realization of emptiness and the complete awakening of wisdom and compassion.

Śāntideva understood this.

The great Mahāyāna aspirations aren't ultimately about making *your* saṃsāra nicer.

They're about ending the ignorance that makes saṃsāra possible while bringing beings toward freedom.

And perhaps that is the greatest thing your geckos have taught you.

You once thought spiritual practice was about obtaining something for yourself.

Cannot admire you.

Cannot increase your social standing.

The compassion itself has become meaningful.

And that is why your third death is different.

Not because the universe owes you something.

Not because the Buddha has secretly placed your name on a celestial VIP list.

But because you've spent a lifetime becoming less dependent upon the idea that everything must happen for *you*\.

**You are still going to die.**

But you are no longer preparing to meet death with nothing except a lifetime of grasping.

And in the Tibetan system, that is an extraordinary beginning.


**Source distinction.** Karma Lingpa's *Bardo Thödol* provides the principal intermediate-state architecture. The *Kāraṇḍavyūha* supplies the early Indian scriptural basis for Avalokiteśvara's six-syllable mantra and its extraordinary efficacy claims. The Sukhāvatī sūtras describe Amitābha's field, while Tibetan masters such as Mipham systematize rebirth aspirations. The sensory-liberation and amulet teachings belong to additional tantric and Dzogchen traditions. These connected sources should not be mistaken for one original text or an empirically verified account of life after death.
