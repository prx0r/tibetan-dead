# Chapter IV

## You Actually Meditated This Time. Unfortunately, You're Still Dead.

*Death yoga, the indestructible mind, the mother and child luminosities, phowa, tukdam, the biological persistence of form, and the extraordinary possibility that you have spent your entire life mistaking the contents of consciousness for its nature.*


## I. You are alive!

Again.

Jesus Christ.

Four human incarnations into this magnificent spiritual disaster, and we are finally making some progress.

Let's review your previous achievements.

In your first life, you were an absolute bastard.

You died, missed the opportunity for liberation, and experienced an extended holiday in the hell realms, whose hospitality was frankly dreadful.

In your second, you discovered Buddhism, accumulated an enormous amount of philosophical information, and then died.

The peaceful Buddhas appeared.

You ran away.

The wrathful Buddhas appeared.

You ran away faster.

The Lord of Death appeared.

You attempted to negotiate.

Eventually you acquired another body.

In your third life, you became compassionate.

You fed geckos.

You learned mantras.

You generated merit.

You dedicated your virtue to all sentient beings, including animals who regarded your spiritual activities as evidence of severe mammalian instability.

You developed bodhicitta and aspirations toward Amitābha's Sukhāvatī.

And then you died.

Again.

But this time, something had changed.

Your compassionate orientation had become habitual.

Your recollection of the Buddha and Dharma was stronger.

Your terror did not completely overwhelm you.

According to the fictional karmic trajectory of our story, the resulting conditions helped bring about another fortunate human birth.

And now here you are.

A little child.

Born into circumstances where Buddhist teachings are accessible.

You possess a reasonably healthy body, enough freedom to practise, and a growing fascination with the nature of awareness.

But you have made one rather important discovery.

**Being a nice person has not actually solved the fundamental problem.**

You still experience fear.

You still experience craving.

You still identify with your thoughts.

You still feel that somewhere, behind the eyes, there must be a little person looking out at the world.

An owner.

A controller.

An enduring subject.

You have spent several lifetimes improving the behaviour of this imaginary executive.

You have made him more compassionate.

More knowledgeable.

Less inclined to cause misery.

Excellent developments.

But you have not yet investigated whether he actually exists in the way you imagine.

And that is what this life is going to be about.

Because, this time, you're not merely going to read about the nature of mind.

**You're going to fucking look.**


## II. At twenty-three, you meet a Tibetan meditation master who has absolutely no interest in your opinions.

You've travelled to a monastery.

You've read extensively.

You understand Madhyamaka.

You've studied dependent origination.

You've developed a reasonably sophisticated interpretation of consciousness.

You are particularly pleased with your ability to explain the difference between conventional and ultimate truth.

You sit before the teacher.

He asks a simple question.

*What is aware of this experience?*

You immediately prepare an elaborate philosophical response.

Something about the absence of an intrinsically existing subject, the dependent arising of cognition, the union of clarity and emptiness, and several important distinctions between Yogācāra and Madhyamaka.

Your teacher listens.

Then asks:

*Right now?*

You begin again.

He interrupts.

*Look.*

You look.

And discover something deeply inconvenient.

**You don't actually know what the fuck he's asking you to do.**

You can describe awareness.

You can classify awareness.

You can compare Buddhist theories of awareness.

You can write a magnificent essay about awareness.

But when someone asks you to attend directly to the knowing of present experience, your mind immediately starts talking about it.

Your teacher points out that you have replaced investigation with commentary.

You are mildly offended.

You spent considerable money on those books.

But he has identified the central difficulty.

You are very good at manipulating the *contents* of consciousness.

Thoughts.

Concepts.

Memories.

Images.

Feelings.

You are much less familiar with investigating the nature of the knowing through which those contents appear.

Now, several Tibetan traditions approach this problem differently.

In Geluk presentations of the completion stage of highest yoga tantra, the subtlest mind is brought into the path through elaborate practices involving winds, channels and the realization of emptiness.

In Mahāmudrā, the practitioner investigates the nature of mind and the inseparability of appearance and emptiness.

In Dzogchen, a qualified teacher may introduce the practitioner to *rigpa*, the knowing of the primordial nature of awareness.

These are related but not interchangeable systems.

**Rigpa is not simply concentration.**

Nor is it an ordinary state of being very alert.

And the subtlest mind of tantric death physiology is not automatically identical, as a doctrinal category, with realized nondual wisdom.

That distinction will become exceptionally important when you die.

For the moment, the teacher gives you a considerably less glamorous assignment.

Sit.

Observe.

Investigate.

And stop congratulating yourself every time you have an interesting thought.

A devastating blow to your spiritual self-esteem.


## III. Dream yoga: You've been dying in miniature every fucking night.

Years pass.

You develop a serious meditation practice.

Attention becomes steadier.

Thoughts remain present, but they no longer command quite the same authority.

You begin noticing a remarkable fact.

A thought arises.

You know it.

The thought disappears.

Another appears.

You know that too.

Pleasure arises.

It disappears.

Fear arises.

It disappears.

The experiences change constantly, but their changing character becomes increasingly obvious.

Now your teacher introduces dream yoga.

You are delighted.

Finally, an advanced practice that involves lying down.

Unfortunately, you quickly discover that you're expected to remain aware rather than simply enjoy an excellent night's sleep.

The idea is straightforward but extraordinarily powerful.

**Recognize a dream as a dream while it is happening.**

You dream that you're standing in a beautiful mountain valley.

The sky is blue.

A magnificent monastery stands on a distant hill.

You begin walking toward it.

Then a tiger appears.

Oh, for fuck's sake.

You run.

The tiger pursues you.

You become terrified.

And then something occurs to you.

*Hang on.*

*I'm asleep.*

The landscape remains.

The tiger remains.

The experience remains vivid.

But the relationship changes.

You are no longer convinced that the tiger possesses the independent reality you previously attributed to it.

And, just for a moment, the fear loses its authority.

This is only an illustrative lucid-dream episode. Traditional dream yoga goes well beyond recognizing that one is dreaming.

Patrul Rinpoche's synopsis of the Six Yogas distinguishes recognizing dreams, purifying their appearances, training in their illusory nature and resting in suchness.

The point is ultimately not to become exceptionally good at manipulating imaginary scenery.

It is to use dreams as a setting for investigating how appearances are known, grasped and mistaken for inherently existing things.

### The tiger is not the ultimate problem.

This is the part you slowly begin to understand.

The ordinary response to the dream tiger is:

*There is a terrifying object over there, and I must escape.*

Lucidity introduces another possibility.

The experience may still be unpleasant.

But the independent existence attributed to the tiger is no longer taken for granted.

And now you examine waking experience.

You see an attractive person.

Desire arises.

You see someone who has insulted you.

Aversion arises.

You remember a personal humiliation.

Shame arises.

These experiences are not simply equivalent to dream hallucinations.

But the mind similarly imposes interpretations, identities and apparently solid distinctions upon what arises.

And ordinary Buddhist analysis goes further: waking phenomena, too, are empty of inherent existence, although their conventional reality and causal effects must not be denied.

The important insight is not that waking life is literally a private dream.

It is that **appearing convincingly is not sufficient evidence for existing independently**.

Now consider death.

The ordinary sensory world dissolves.

The familiar body is no longer available as the centre of experience.

And, according to the bardo teachings, powerful appearances can arise.

If you are completely accustomed to believing whatever appears, you will be frightened or attracted according to your karmic conditioning.

But if you have repeatedly practised recognizing appearances without grasping at their independent existence, your response may be different.

Dream yoga is therefore one form of preparation.

Not because the bardo is scientifically known to be a dream.

But because the Tibetan traditions describe both as states in which recognition may liberate experience from habitual misidentification.

Now your teacher adds sleep yoga.

And you discover that things are about to become considerably more subtle.


## IV. Sleep yoga: Apparently even unconsciousness is no excuse.

Ordinary dreams are full of appearances.

People.

Places.

Objects.

Stories.

But what about the transition into sleep?

The world of waking sensation recedes.

Thought becomes less coherent.

Ordinary self-location weakens.

And eventually, in deep dreamless sleep, the autobiographical and perceptual activity familiar from waking life may be absent or unavailable to later recall.

Now certain Tibetan tantric systems claim that trained practitioners can become familiar with an extraordinarily subtle luminosity associated with sleep.

This is not simply remembering a dream after waking.

Nor is it merely remaining physically awake.

The aim is to recognize increasingly subtle modes of awareness as coarser mental activity subsides.

The traditional analogy is explicit.

Sleep resembles death.

Dreaming resembles an intermediate state.

Waking resembles rebirth.

But the analogy concerns the pattern of dissolution and re-emergence, not an assertion that ordinary sleeping people literally become medically dead every night.

Your teacher introduces the broader Six Yogas framework.

The practices associated with Nāropa include inner heat, illusory body, dream yoga, luminosity, intermediate-state practice and transference, although enumerations and classifications differ among lineages.

These practices collectively concern the relationship between subtle embodiment, experience and liberation.

Some train with dreams.

Some concern the recognition of luminosity.

Some involve elaborate completion-stage practices relating consciousness to the subtle winds.

And some concern what happens when the present life actually ends.

The crucial point is that the methods are not merely different supernatural tricks.

They are attempts to bring experiences ordinarily governed by ignorance onto the path.

Patrul Rinpoche's synopsis describes luminosity in terms of ground, path and fruition, and identifies sleep, meditative absorption and death as important occasions for its recognition.

You spend years becoming familiar with increasingly subtle mental activity.

You develop confidence.

And then your teacher says something that thoroughly destroys your confidence.

*Even if you can remain aware during sleep, that does not mean you have realized emptiness.*

Ah.

Right.

Because this is the distinction that keeps returning.

A subtle state is still a state.

An unusual experience is still an experience.

A brilliant luminous appearance may still be grasped as an independently existing phenomenon.

And if ignorance remains, the basic problem remains.

**You can become astonishingly skilled at navigating consciousness while still misunderstanding its nature.**

This will matter enormously at the final moment.

Because you are now going to learn what Tibetan tantric physiology claims happens to consciousness when the organism dies.


## V. The subtle body: You have apparently got seventy-two thousand invisible pipes.

You're now forty-one.

Your teacher begins explaining the subtle-body system of highest yoga tantra.

You are immediately suspicious.

He tells you that consciousness is connected with *rlung* - wind, or *prāṇa* in Sanskrit.

He describes subtle channels, *rtsa* or Sanskrit *nāḍī*.

He speaks of drops or essences, *thig le*, corresponding to Sanskrit *bindu* in these contexts.

And he explains that ordinary cognitive activity is intimately associated with the movement of subtle winds.

You ask:

*So where are these channels?*

He gestures toward the body.

*Can we find them in an anatomy textbook?*

Not in the ordinary sense.

*Can I get them scanned?*

That would not establish the traditional subtle channels.

*Are you telling me I have an entire system of invisible plumbing?*

In a way.

You are becoming concerned that Buddhist metaphysics has become an enormously complicated plumbing manual.

### What are the winds supposed to be?

The important thing is not to translate *rlung* straight into electricity.

Or oxygen.

Or electromagnetic fields.

Or the autonomic nervous system.

Tibetan tantric physiology describes winds as subtle energetic supports or vehicles of consciousness.

Ordinary and extraordinary mental states are associated with configurations of these winds.

And at death, their progressive dissolution is said to correspond with the cessation of coarser cognitive activity.

In important highest-yoga-tantra systems, the subtle mind and its associated wind are inseparable in a very specific doctrinal sense.

The mind is the knowing aspect.

The wind is its subtle energetic vehicle.

They are distinguishable conceptually, but related so intimately that consciousness is described as riding upon wind, rather like a rider upon a horse.

The imagery is traditional, not a measurement of two experimentally identified substances.

### The three principal channels

Within one widespread presentation, there is a central channel and two lateral channels.

The ordinary movement of winds through the lateral channels is associated with the functioning of conceptual cognition.

The practices of the completion stage seek to induce winds to enter, abide and dissolve within the central channel, enabling increasingly subtle states of consciousness.

This is a profoundly different goal from conventional breathing exercises.

The central channel isn't simply a nerve.

The right and left channels aren't anatomical blood vessels.

And the subtle winds cannot currently be identified with measurable electrical or metabolic processes in any scientifically established one-to-one correspondence.

Their details also vary among tantric systems.

But what is the internal logic?

Ordinary mental activity depends upon particular configurations of winds.

Change those configurations, and the corresponding mental states change.

At death, those configurations dissolve naturally.

The accomplished practitioner attempts to use that dissolution for realization.

This is why certain completion-stage yogas involve working with the winds while alive.

**They are rehearsing, within a traditional subtle-body framework, the conditions under which the mind is believed to become exceptionally subtle at death.**

Lati Rinbochay and Jeffrey Hopkins's *Death, Intermediate State, and Rebirth in Tibetan Buddhism*, based on Geluk presentations associated with Tsongkhapa, offers a detailed explanation of this physiology. It associates the dissolution of the eighty coarse conceptions with the withdrawal of winds and describes the progressively subtler appearances culminating in clear light. 

### The eighty conceptions

Now your teacher tells you there are eighty natural conceptions associated with ordinary mental activity.

You immediately reach for a notebook.

He looks exhausted.

This isn't principally an opportunity to accumulate another impressive list.

The important claim is that ordinary conceptual consciousness has a complex supporting structure.

When the subtle winds associated with those mental processes dissolve, corresponding forms of cognition cease manifesting.

The eighty conceptions are classified in traditional tantric analysis in relation to the three more subtle minds of appearance, increase and near-attainment.

Then those three themselves cease to dominate.

And the clear-light mind manifests.

You have learned something genuinely consequential.

**In this system, the death process isn't simply a series of increasingly interesting visual effects. It's a progressive withdrawal of the conditions sustaining ordinary dualistic cognition.**

Now you ask the crucial question.

*If all my ordinary thoughts disappear, what remains?*

And your teacher replies:

*That is precisely what you need to understand.*


## VI. The indestructible drop: Have the Tibetans located consciousness in the heart?

Now the metaphysics gets extremely subtle.

Within the Geluk highest-yoga-tantra account, the heart centre plays a crucial role in the dissolution process.

The teachings speak of an indestructible drop, associated with the extremely subtle wind and mind.

The term *indestructible* does not mean that there is a tiny physically indestructible marble lodged in the cardiac muscle.

It belongs to the traditional subtle-body description of the basis of continuity through life, death and rebirth.

The key doctrinal proposition is that the subtlest wind and mind endure as a continuum until liberation, while the grosser forms of embodied cognition arise and cease.

But what does *endure* mean here?

Not that an eternal soul exists.

Buddhist philosophy still rejects an independently existing permanent self.

The continuum is understood to be changing and dependently arisen.

Imagine a flame.

It is sustained by a succession of physical conditions.

The flame at one moment is not numerically identical to the flame at another.

Yet the process is causally continuous.

The tantric system proposes a still subtler kind of continuity, in which mind and its energetic vehicle remain associated through the transitions of death and rebirth.

It is not the physical persistence of the old brain.

Nor is it the migration of an unchanging person.

The continuity consists of conditioned processes.

Now you raise the obvious objection.

*If consciousness depends upon a physical brain, how could a mental continuum continue when that brain decomposes?*

Excellent.

The Buddhist account must deny that the mind's causal dependence is exhausted by the gross neural structures of this particular body.

It proposes a more subtle supporting basis.

But modern neuroscience has not established such a basis.

Indeed, extensive evidence links consciousness, memory, personality and cognitive capacities to brain function.

Brain injury can alter them.

Anesthesia can disrupt them.

Neurodegeneration can progressively destroy their ordinary expression.

The Tibetan hypothesis is therefore not a trivial extension of established neuroscience.

It's a substantive metaphysical alternative concerning the nature and continuity of consciousness.

And if someone wants to connect the two, they have substantial explanatory work to do.

Your teacher, meanwhile, is not offering an account of synaptic transmission.

He's explaining how the tantric path uses its own model of mind and embodiment.

Now the heart-centre doctrine becomes important again.

At the culmination of the death process, the subtle winds are described as dissolving into their most fundamental support.

The coarser mental functions cease manifesting.

And the subtlest mind becomes accessible.

This is the heart of death yoga.

But it comes with a tremendous philosophical trap.

### The subtlest mind is not automatically enlightened.

You are delighted to hear that the clear-light mind manifests at death.

*Fantastic!*

*So everyone gets enlightened!*

No.

*But everyone's ordinary conceptual activity dissolves?*

Within this account, yes.

*And the clear light manifests?*

Yes.

*So what's the fucking problem?*

**Recognizing it.**

The ordinary clear light of death is not the same accomplishment as a fully realized wisdom consciousness directly knowing emptiness.

Lati Rinbochay and Hopkins explicitly distinguish the clear light arising naturally for ordinary beings from the fully qualified clear light cultivated and realized by a yogi.

Their Geluk account is especially precise: the mere cessation of coarse dualistic appearances does not constitute realization of the absence of inherent existence.

If it did, every sentient being would become liberated simply by dying.

And we would have no more saṃsāra to worry about.

This is absolutely central.

A mind can become subtle without becoming wise.

An experience can become nonconceptual without realizing emptiness.

You can enter an extraordinary state and still fail to understand its nature.

This is why death yoga involves both training in subtle states and the cultivation of wisdom.

It isn't enough to manipulate the energetic configuration.

You must know what you're doing philosophically.

Otherwise, you've simply become a remarkably accomplished technician of your own confusion.


## VII. Mother and child luminosity: The metaphysical family reunion of the century

At fifty, you've been practising seriously for decades.

Your teacher introduces one of the most beautiful metaphors in Tibetan contemplative literature.

**The meeting of mother and child luminosity.**

It sounds like a religious family reunion.

Perhaps two luminous relatives embrace.

Perhaps a grandmother emerges from a rainbow.

Perhaps there is tea.

Not quite.

The basic distinction is between the luminosity associated with the ground or fundamental nature and the recognition cultivated through the path.

The mother luminosity is the ground luminosity.

The child luminosity is the path luminosity: the practitioner’s cultivated familiarity with the nature of mind.

At death, when the ground luminosity manifests, the practitioner who has developed stable recognition can recognize it.

The two are said to meet.

But this is not the joining of two separate minds.

Nor is it the reunion of your individual consciousness with some enormous divine consciousness that owns the universe.

The metaphor indicates recognition of what was always the nature of experience.

The child recognizes the mother because the nature encountered at death is not fundamentally foreign to what has been recognized through practice.

In the Nyingma *Liberation Upon Hearing* by Mindroling Terchen, the convergence is explicitly associated with the possibility of awakening as clear-light dharmakāya at death.

### What precisely meets what?

Imagine that every experience you have ever had is characterized by two features.

It appears.

And it lacks independent, self-existing essence.

The ordinary ignorant mind grasps its appearances as intrinsically real.

The practitioner investigates.

Through meditation and wisdom, the empty nature of appearances becomes increasingly familiar.

In Dzogchen language, the practitioner is introduced to rigpa and learns to recognize it.

Now, at death, the ordinary structures of experience dissolve.

The ground luminosity dawns.

And the practitioner recognizes the nature with which they have become familiar.

Mother and child meet.

But that raises a subtle question.

If the ground luminosity is always present as the nature of awareness, why does it become especially significant at death?

Because, in the traditional explanation, the dissolution of coarser mental activity removes many of the ordinary obscuring processes.

The fundamental nature is not newly manufactured.

The conditions for recognizing it may become unusually powerful.

However, an untrained mind can pass through this without liberating insight.

So death is both an opportunity and a catastrophe.

The best possible moment to recognize the nature of mind.

And, for most beings, a moment when their habitual ignorance is extraordinarily difficult to interrupt.

### Your teacher makes the problem unbearably clear.

You say:

*I think I understand.*

He asks:

*What is it that you understand?*

You say:

*The mind is empty and luminous.*

He asks:

*Is that your present recognition, or another idea about recognition?*

Oh, for fuck's sake.

You look again.

And you begin to see how frequently you insert a conceptual observer into every experience.

You are aware.

Then you imagine yourself as the owner of awareness.

You notice a thought.

Then you imagine yourself as a separate watcher possessing that thought.

You investigate emptiness.

Then you make emptiness into a special object you hope to acquire.

It's astonishing.

Every time you approach the teaching, the ego arrives with a clipboard and offers to manage the process.

But the deepest recognition does not consist of an independent observer standing apart from phenomena and declaring them empty.

It challenges the whole construction.

And now, after decades of practice, you are developing a familiarity with the distinction between thoughts and their empty, knowing nature.

Not perfect mastery.

Not complete Buddhahood.

But something more substantial than your second life's enormous collection of books.

You are becoming a genuine practitioner.

Which means the next question is unavoidable.

**What are you actually going to do when death arrives?**


## VIII. Phowa: The emergency exit from saṃsāra apparently goes through the roof.

You are now sixty-two.

Your teacher introduces *phowa* - Tibetan *'pho ba*, the transference of consciousness.

You're immediately interested.

*Right.*

*So I can escape through the top of my skull?*

Well, some forms of phowa use the image of the crown aperture, the *brahmarandhra*, and the transference of consciousness toward a Buddha-field.

*Excellent.*

*So after years of trying to understand emptiness, you're telling me there's a fucking exit hatch?*

Not exactly.

Phowa is not just one technique with one outcome.

And it certainly isn't simply a matter of making consciousness fly vertically through physical space.

### The basic transference model

In widely practised forms of phowa, especially those oriented toward Amitābha's Sukhāvatī, the practitioner prepares for the moment of death through devotion, aspiration and specific tantric methods.

The traditional subtle-body explanation associates consciousness with winds and channels, and certain practices describe its transfer through the crown aperture toward a pure realm.

But the doctrinal claim concerns the transition of the mental continuum, not a brain-shaped object physically escaping through the skull.

You ask:

*Could someone actually transfer my consciousness for me?*

Some traditions hold that an accomplished practitioner can perform phowa on behalf of a dying or deceased person, under appropriate conditions.

Again, that is a traditional claim, not a demonstrated medical or physical intervention.

*And what if it goes wrong?*

Well, this is precisely why legitimate traditions distinguish preparation, competence, timing and guidance.

These are advanced death-oriented tantric practices.

They are not instructions for manipulating respiration, forcing bodily processes or attempting to precipitate death.

Our task here is to understand their metaphysics, not to perform them.

### The extraordinary complication: the highest phowa doesn't actually go anywhere.

Now this is brilliant.

You read Karma Chakmé's commentary on consciousness transference.

And discover that he distinguishes forms of phowa associated with different levels of realization.

At the highest level, *dharmakāya phowa* involves recognizing the nature of mind.

There is no ultimately existing person being ejected from the body.

No independent destination to travel toward.

No actual metaphysical projectile.

The highest transference is free from the conceptual division between someone transferring and something being transferred.

Other forms involve aspiration toward pure realms and the methods associated with the *saṃbhogakāya* or *nirmāṇakāya*.

Karma Chakmé's text explicitly connects the highest form with Mahāmudrā and Dzogchen *trekchö* and the meeting of mother and child luminosity.

Do you see the philosophical elegance?

At one level, the problem is framed as moving consciousness from an unfavourable condition to a favourable one.

At a deeper level, the problem is recognizing the empty nature of consciousness and the apparent mover.

The highest liberation does not involve successfully relocating an immortal ego.

It involves the realization that the ego was never independently established.

### Different exits, different destinations

Your teacher explains that the practical purpose of phowa depends on the form of the practice.

One person may aspire to rebirth in Sukhāvatī.

Another may employ completion-stage practices associated with the realization of the nature of mind.

A more accomplished practitioner may already have stabilized recognition and require no imagined movement toward a separate destination.

Longchenpa's *Lama Yangtik Phowa* presents an explicitly Dzogchen-associated transference instruction, while other Tibetan traditions preserve distinct liturgies and classifications.

These should not be compressed into one supposedly universal technique. 

Now you have several possible ways to approach death.

If you recognize the clear light, liberation may occur.

If you cannot sustain that recognition, devotion and phowa may support a favourable transition according to their respective traditions.

If those methods are unavailable or ineffective, the bardo instructions may provide further opportunities.

And if you fail to recognize those?

Karma, habituation and aspiration still condition the next existence.

This is the extraordinary structure of Tibetan death practice.

**It is not one technique. It is a hierarchy of liberating opportunities adapted to different capacities.**

And you're finally developing some of those capacities.

Which is fortunate.

Because at seventy-eight, you discover that you are dying.

This time, the prospect does not merely terrify you.

You have rehearsed the dissolution of ordinary experience.

You have become familiar with the nature of mind.

You have trained in the recognition of appearances.

And you have spent decades cultivating compassion.

You are ready to confront the moment that defeated you three times.

Or, more precisely, you're as ready as you can reasonably be.

Because the universe has an unfortunate habit of refusing to provide a practice examination before the final one.


## IX. You are dying. And for once, you know what the fuck is happening.

You're seventy-eight.

The doctors have explained that the disease is advanced.

They have done what medicine can reasonably do.

There is no further curative treatment.

Your body is failing.

But your mind is relatively clear.

And now something extraordinary happens.

You are no longer asking why death is happening to you.

You've known for decades that it would.

The question is different.

**Can you remain familiar with the nature of awareness as the ordinary supports of experience disappear?**

You arrange for appropriate medical and palliative care.

Your teacher and companions know your wishes.

They help maintain a peaceful environment.

You don't refuse needed treatment to prove your spiritual accomplishments.

Nor do you assume that an undisturbed death must be free from pain medication or medical intervention.

You understand that practical compassion includes relieving suffering.

Then, when the time comes, you begin to recollect the teachings.

And the ancient Tibetan description becomes astonishingly immediate.

### First dissolution: Earth into water

Your body grows heavy.

Movement becomes difficult.

Your senses weaken.

The boundaries of ordinary bodily experience become less reliable.

In the traditional account, an inner appearance resembling a mirage may arise.

You remember what your teacher said.

The associated functions of the earth element are dissolving.

Not that your bones are literally turning into water.

Not that your tissues have spontaneously abandoned chemistry.

The tantric explanation describes the failure of the bodily and energetic conditions associated with solidity and stability.

You remain as attentive as you can.

### Second dissolution: Water into fire

Moisture-associated bodily functions weaken.

Your mouth becomes dry.

Your ordinary sensory capacities decline.

The traditional inner appearance is compared to smoke.

You know the description.

And, crucially, you have practised responding to unfamiliar experiences without immediately becoming their prisoner.

You are frightened.

But you don't have to interpret fear as a command.

### Third dissolution: Fire into wind

Body warmth diminishes.

The ordinary physiological functions associated with heat and digestion fail.

The corresponding inner appearance is likened to sparks or fireflies.

Here, the traditional description becomes particularly vivid.

The body which has sustained every ordinary experience of your life is ceasing to function.

Every familiar relationship between attention, sensation and movement is changing.

And yet your training directs you away from desperate attempts to preserve a fixed identity.

The body is impermanent.

You have known this for years.

Now it is no longer a philosophical proposition.

It is what is happening.

### Fourth dissolution: Wind into consciousness

Breathing becomes increasingly laboured.

Finally, the gross breath ceases.

The traditional internal appearance is compared to a flickering butter lamp, after which the coarse modes of cognition subside.

Now we must be extremely precise.

**A clinician declaring death and a tantric practitioner describing the dissolution of subtle consciousness are not measuring the same thing.**

Cardiac arrest, permanent cessation of circulation, respiratory arrest and death determined by neurological criteria are medically distinct concepts with specific clinical standards.

Some circulatory arrests are reversible through resuscitation.

Established death is not something a person can reverse through meditation.

The Tibetan account places additional stages beyond the cessation of gross respiration, but those are contemplative descriptions not established clinical measurements.

Tenzin Namdul's 2025 ethnographic study describes how Tibetan teachers understand the eight stages and why they distinguish the cessation of coarser physiological functions from the completion of the subtle death process.

Now, in our fictional account, the ordinary embodied processes have ceased.

Your community recognizes your death.

And the subtle sequence, as described by the tradition, continues.

### Fifth dissolution: White appearance

The winds supporting the coarse conceptual processes have subsided.

The white essence associated symbolically and physiologically with the paternal contribution is said to descend toward the heart.

The corresponding consciousness experiences an extraordinary white appearance.

Like a vast autumn sky suffused with moonlight.

Not an object you see through your physical eyes.

The visual organs are no longer performing their ordinary functions.

This is an inner appearance within the traditional subtle-mind account.

The ordinary discursive mind is absent.

But the experience is not yet the ultimate clear light.

### Sixth dissolution: Red increase

The red essence associated with the maternal contribution is said to ascend toward the heart.

An extraordinary red appearance manifests.

Traditionally compared to an autumn sky illuminated by sunlight or a red glow.

The mind becomes subtler still.

The ordinary conceptual distinctions continue to disappear.

You're not trying to remember the shopping list.

You're not attempting to move your legs.

You're not constructing an argument about the best translation of *śūnyatā*.

The cognitive processes through which those activities ordinarily occur are no longer manifesting.

### Seventh dissolution: Black near-attainment

The white and red essences converge in the heart-centre account.

The mind experiences darkness.

There may be a phase described as a loss of conscious apprehension.

And then the deepest subtle condition is said to manifest.

Notice the distinction.

A disappearance of ordinary conceptuality isn't identical to the cessation of every possible mode of knowing within this Buddhist account.

The tradition describes a subtler continuity beneath the processes familiar from waking experience.

### Eighth dissolution: Clear light

Finally.

The ground clear light.

No ordinary sensory world.

No familiar narrative self.

No internal commentary explaining how magnificently you're handling the experience.

The subtlest mind manifests.

The traditional distinction is between merely undergoing this state and recognizing its nature.

The entire purpose of your decades of training is concentrated here.

And the question is no longer whether you remember the theory.

**Does the recognition cultivated during life remain operative when the structures supporting ordinary cognition have disappeared?**

This is the meeting of mother and child luminosity.

The ground luminosity.

The path's familiar recognition.

No two physical things collide.

No cosmic mother literally embraces a glowing infant.

Recognition is the issue.

And if it occurs with genuine realization, the tradition describes a possibility of liberation at death.

If it does not, the karmic processes leading to the intermediate states can continue.

The sequence of the four internal appearances and the white and red drops is laid out particularly explicitly in Geluk-oriented presentations of death yoga. It should not be assumed that every school gives the same anatomical and contemplative explanation. 

Now, our fictional practitioner has entered this final transition.

What precisely happens subjectively cannot be observed by the people gathered around the body.

They do not possess direct access to the alleged subtlest consciousness.

And that limitation takes us to one of the most extraordinary topics in the entire series.

Because some Tibetan practitioners are said to remain in meditation after clinical death.

And their bodies sometimes appear to decay unusually slowly.

Which means we need to introduce the scientists.


## X. You're dead. The monks are delighted. The scientists are deeply confused.

You have died.

Your heartbeat has ceased.

Your breathing has stopped.

The doctors have fulfilled their clinical responsibilities.

Your body is resting in a dignified position.

The monastery has been informed.

Your companions observe the customs appropriate to your training and wishes.

And then, according to our fictional narrative, something unusual happens.

Your body does not exhibit the expected outward progression of decomposition.

Not immediately.

Nor as rapidly as some observers would anticipate over the following days.

People begin discussing the possibility of *tukdam*.

Tibetan:

Wylie: *thugs dam*.

A term associated with meditative commitment or contemplative absorption, which in this context designates a reported postmortem meditative state.

Traditional observers may look for an unexpectedly fresh appearance, unusual suppleness, persistence of posture, absence of pronounced putrefactive odour, or particular patterns of retained warmth.

Accounts differ, and none of these signs alone establishes the presence of consciousness.

Within the religious interpretation, a trained practitioner may be resting in an extremely subtle state of awareness associated with the completion of the death process.

This could continue after the outward cessation of ordinary physiological activity.

And, in some traditional accounts, the subtle consciousness has not yet departed.

Now you might imagine that modern scientists would approach this topic by immediately proclaiming either:

*Miracle! Consciousness survives death!*

Or:

*Absolute nonsense! Everyone go home!*

Fortunately, the actual research is considerably more interesting.

Because the investigators have been asking two distinct questions.

**First: is there measurable residual brain activity?**

**Second: are the reported patterns of delayed decomposition genuinely unusual and scientifically characterizable?**

These questions are related, but they are not identical.

And they have produced importantly different findings.

### Before we examine the evidence, one distinction.

*Delayed decomposition is not equivalent to suspended biological death.*

A body can retain a recognizable appearance after death.

Different tissues decay at different rates.

Environmental conditions can slow or accelerate their breakdown.

The appearance of freshness is not proof that cellular metabolism continues normally.

Nor is the absence of an obvious smell proof that the brain is conscious.

So even a genuinely unusual preservation pattern would not, by itself, establish that awareness continues after death.

But it could still represent a scientifically interesting phenomenon requiring explanation.

And that is exactly why serious researchers have investigated it.


## XI. The tukdam EEG study: The scientists find absolutely fuck-all.

In 2021, Dylan Lott, Richard Davidson, Antoine Lutz, John Dunne, Tibetan collaborators and a larger research team published a study in *Frontiers in Psychology*.

Its title was unusually explicit:

*No Detectable Electroencephalographic Activity After Clinical Declaration of Death Among Tibetan Buddhist Meditators in Apparent Tukdam, a Putative Postmortem Meditation State.*

Not exactly a subtle headline.

The researchers investigated whether people recognized by Tibetan communities as being in tukdam exhibited measurable electrophysiological activity after clinical death.

They recruited fourteen living subjects for baseline measurements and recorded thirteen postmortem subjects.

Of the thirteen postmortem recordings, six were excluded from the relevant analysis because of excessive electrical interference or incomplete protocols, leaving seven analysable cases.

The researchers used EEG and tests of brain responses to auditory stimulation.

Their living-subject recordings established that recognizable signals could be detected despite difficult field conditions.

What did they find in the deceased subjects?

**No recognizable EEG activity.**

No convincing auditory brainstem responses.

No detectable event-related brain responses of the types investigated.

The earliest postmortem recording began twenty-six hours after clinical death.

Other recordings commonly began three to five days afterward.

The study therefore did not investigate the first minutes or hours of the death process. 

And that distinction is absolutely essential.

### What does this result actually mean?

Imagine you're a scientist.

Your hypothesis is that some residual organized neural activity may persist in these postmortem cases.

You bring electrodes.

You record.

And you find no recognizable activity.

You cannot reasonably announce:

*Excellent. We have demonstrated that consciousness is continuing invisibly.*

You haven't.

Your actual measurement has failed to detect the relevant neural signal.

But nor does the experiment establish that absolutely no form of awareness could exist under any metaphysical interpretation.

EEG detects particular patterns of electrical activity generated by neural populations.

It does not measure consciousness as a substance.

And if a religious tradition explicitly proposes a kind of awareness that is not dependent upon the ordinary functioning of the brain, EEG cannot automatically settle the entire philosophical question.

However, there's a serious problem for the religious interpretation if it predicts measurable ongoing neural functioning.

The data provide no support for that prediction in the cases and periods tested.

The researchers themselves distinguished the possibility of residual brain activity from the broader, much harder question of postmortem awareness.

This was a study of measurable electrophysiology.

It was not a direct test of whether a nonphysical continuum can survive death.

### Why the twenty-six-hour delay matters

A brain can exhibit unusual electrical activity during the dying process.

And transient neural activity near cardiac arrest is not the same thing as sustained activity days after death.

In a separate 2023 study published in *PNAS*, researchers examined four comatose patients as life support was withdrawn.

Two exhibited substantial increases in gamma-frequency activity and measures of neural connectivity as their physiological condition deteriorated.

These findings were striking.

But neither patient could subsequently report subjective experiences.

And the study did not demonstrate consciousness persisting for days after death.

That gives us three distinct propositions.

A brain can exhibit transient organized activity near death.

A practitioner may, according to Tibetan tradition, enter an exceptionally subtle meditative condition during death.

And a body may exhibit delayed decomposition after death.

**These are three different claims.**

You cannot establish the third by demonstrating the first.

Nor can you establish the second merely by observing the third.

The original tukdam EEG investigators were unusually careful about this.

They acknowledged that their measurements began too late to address the earliest postmortem period.

And that remains one of the major challenges in the research.

According to the project's March 2026 update, investigators still had not succeeded in prospectively observing a participant through the complete perimortem transition under their proposed second-phase study design.

So, scientifically, the question of what occurs during the earliest transition remains incompletely investigated in this particular population.

Your fictional body, meanwhile, is lying there.

The monks are contemplating the meaning of the death.

The scientists are examining their recordings.

And someone has just asked the question that is going to make everything much more interesting.

*What about the decomposition?*

Because, strangely enough, that's where the strongest concrete observations have emerged.


## XII. Thirty-eight days without ordinary decomposition? Now we're talking.

In 2024, Tawni Tidwell and colleagues published a forensic study in *Forensic Science International: Reports*.

The title was:

Delayed decompositional changes in indoor settings among Tibetan monastic communities in India: A case report.

The paper examined two particularly prolonged cases from Tibetan monasteries in India.

One was followed until thirty-eight days after clinical death.

The other until twenty-seven days.

And, unlike vague stories about bodies mysteriously remaining perfect, the researchers documented actual changes in colour, skin integrity, surface temperature, humidity and visible decomposition.

Forensic specialists from multiple countries participated.

This was a real observational study.

And its conclusions deserve serious attention.

But first, we must correct a very tempting misunderstanding.

**Thirty-eight days after death does not mean thirty-eight days with absolutely no decomposition.**

In the thirty-eight-day case, the first observed green abdominal discolouration and skin marbling appeared around day twelve.

Changes then progressed.

Facial desiccation was observed later, with external sunlight likely contributing to drying and partial mummification.

A putrefactive odour was not noted until approximately day thirty-seven.

By day thirty-eight, much more pronounced changes had developed.

So this wasn't a biologically pristine body that suddenly decomposed after a month.

It was a body showing unusually slow and unevenly distributed externally observable decomposition, followed by later acceleration.

The authors considered the pattern significant relative to expectations for the environmental circumstances.

In the twenty-seven-day case, signs of decomposition were already visible by day eight.

But progression was slow.

The study reported an extended period of relatively limited change, followed by more rapid desiccation and other postmortem alterations.

No insect activity was noted during the observation period.

Putrefactive odour was detected on day twenty-seven.

These are not fairy tales about an immortal corpse.

They're specific forensic observations with dates, measurements and documented limitations.

### Why is that interesting?

Because decomposition normally begins through a complex combination of processes.

Cells lose the capacity to sustain homeostasis.

Membrane integrity fails.

Enzymes begin breaking down cellular structures.

Bacteria multiply and change the tissue environment.

Microbial fermentation and putrefaction produce gases and other chemical products.

Insects may colonize accessible tissues.

Temperature, humidity, clothing, ventilation, medical history and body composition all influence the rate.

The process is neither instantaneous nor uniform.

Different tissues can remain structurally recognizable for different periods after organismal death.

The central question is therefore not:

*Why isn't the body turning to dust immediately?*

No forensic scientist expects that.

The question is:

**Why do some observed bodies appear to change so slowly compared with what would be anticipated under apparently comparable conditions?**

The researchers considered the two cases sufficiently unusual to justify further investigation.

But the paper was a case report, not a controlled experiment demonstrating that meditation caused the delay.

That limitation is crucial.

The two prolonged cases were specifically selected for detailed presentation.

They were not a random sample proving that all trained meditators decompose unusually slowly.

Nor did the published report establish that their preservation was caused by consciousness remaining in the body.

The study makes the case for further research into the factors affecting postmortem decomposition.

It does not resolve the mechanism.

### By March 2026, the research programme had documented sixty-one cases.

That's the figure reported by the Tukdam Project.

But don't misunderstand the number.

Sixty-one documented cases does not mean sixty-one experimentally proven instances of postmortem consciousness.

It means the project has documented people recognized within participating communities as having entered the relevant state.

The published EEG and forensic studies address smaller subsets.

The programme has been expanding its forensic and physiological measurements, but its own study description acknowledges that measurements during the dying transition remain difficult to obtain.

And now we arrive at the exciting problem.

Because we have a phenomenon worthy of investigation.

But we do not yet have a demonstrated causal explanation.

Which means we need to examine the biological possibilities.

And this is where the apparently miraculous body becomes a genuinely fascinating scientific object.


## XIII. What actually keeps a corpse fresh? Let's investigate the fucking corpse.

Right.

We have a body.

We have an unexpectedly slow progression of visible decomposition.

We have no convincing evidence of ongoing ordinary neural activity in the published postmortem EEG cases.

And we have a religious tradition proposing that subtle consciousness can remain associated with the body after the cessation of gross physiological functions.

Now let's approach this like scientists rather than exceptionally enthusiastic mystics.

### Hypothesis one: Temperature

Temperature is one of the most important determinants of decomposition.

A cool environment can slow enzymatic reactions, microbial activity and insect development.

A warmer environment generally accelerates them, although the relationships become more complicated when drying, oxygen availability and other factors change.

The first 2024 tukdam case had an average recorded indoor air temperature of approximately 21°C during observation.

That's not refrigeration.

But nor is it equivalent to a body exposed in the open at 35°C.

The room's actual temperature and humidity matter enormously.

So do airflow, sunlight, insulation, clothing and contact with surrounding surfaces.

A corpse indoors under particular coverings can behave quite differently from one exposed to the elements.

One cannot simply say:

*It's India, therefore it must be boiling, therefore this is miraculous.*

India is not a single climatic condition.

And even within one room, different bodily surfaces experience different environments.

The forensic authors recorded ambient and surface temperatures precisely because these factors are potentially explanatory.

But temperature alone may not adequately explain every observation.

And that is what makes the cases worth investigating.

### Hypothesis two: Microbes

Now things get particularly interesting.

You might imagine decomposition as a process performed entirely by bacteria entering from outside.

But the human body already contains enormous microbial communities.

Especially in the gastrointestinal tract.

During life, the immune system, tissue barriers, chemical environments and other physiological controls influence where these organisms can thrive.

After death, these controls deteriorate.

Microbes may proliferate, migrate and contribute to tissue breakdown.

Different microbial communities can produce different decomposition trajectories.

Medication history matters.

Antibiotic exposure may matter.

Disease matters.

The physiological condition of the person before death may matter.

And local environmental microbiology matters.

The term *necrobiome* is sometimes used to describe the ecological community associated with decomposing remains.

A 2024 review of decomposition research emphasized how microbes, insects, drugs, toxins and volatile organic compounds contribute to variation in the rate and character of decay.

Now you see a plausible research direction.

Could some tukdam cases possess unusual pre-death or post-death microbial conditions?

Possibly.

But have we demonstrated that decades of meditation produce a distinctive microbial configuration that significantly slows decomposition?

No.

That would require careful comparative evidence.

You'd need microbial sampling, relevant medical histories, environmental measurements and suitable controls.

The Tukdam Project lists oral microbiome analysis and measurements of volatile compounds as developing components of its research programme.

Those proposed measurements are promising, but they are not already-established explanations of the observed preservation.

### Hypothesis three: Drying and partial mummification

Here is another complication.

Moisture matters profoundly to decomposition.

Under certain conditions, tissues may dry sufficiently to inhibit ordinary microbial breakdown.

This can preserve the external form of parts of the body, even though living physiology has ceased.

And different regions can dry at different rates.

In the thirty-eight-day tukdam case, the researchers noted facial desiccation and suggested that sunlight contributed to differential drying of the face and head.

This matters because a relatively recognizable appearance is not necessarily evidence that living biological organization remains intact.

A preserved face isn't a functioning face.

A recognizable hand isn't an energetically active hand.

A body can retain structure without retaining the processes that once maintained that structure.

But equally, the researchers reported a pattern they regarded as slower than expected, even after considering the environmental conditions.

The proper conclusion is not *mummification explains everything*.

It is that different processes may contribute to the observed preservation.

### Hypothesis four: Insects and physical barriers

This one sounds almost insultingly mundane.

But forensic science doesn't care whether an explanation is glamorous.

Insect access affects decomposition.

Clothing can affect access.

Room construction matters.

Openings in the body matter.

In the reported cases, cotton was placed in the mouth and nostrils as part of local practices.

The researchers explicitly considered how this could influence fluid discharge and insect access.

The second case showed no observed insect activity during the study.

And that's potentially significant.

**You cannot conclude that a body is resisting decomposition through supernatural power without accounting for the organisms normally involved in decomposition.**

Now imagine a scientist arriving at a monastery.

The body appears unusually well preserved.

The monk says:

*He is abiding in the clear light.*

The scientist asks:

*Could I see the temperature records and insect observations?*

The monk is mildly disappointed.

The scientist is being entirely reasonable.

Because a testable mechanism must survive the elimination of ordinary confounding explanations.

But the scientist must also remain open to finding something unusual.

Dismissing observations before properly investigating them isn't scientific either.

### Hypothesis five: The body's condition before death

Now we reach a more intriguing possibility.

Could the life someone lived affect the biological characteristics of their body at death?

Absolutely.

Diet, illness, medication, stress, metabolism, body composition, inflammatory processes and many other factors can influence physiological state.

Meditative training can also affect stress regulation and other measurable physiological variables during life, though the magnitude and nature of effects depend on the practice and the study.

But this establishes only a possible research pathway.

It does not establish that meditation causes prolonged postmortem preservation.

You would have to distinguish the effects of meditation from the effects of monastic lifestyle, diet, medical history, environmental conditions and selection bias.

And the study would need to investigate whether any pre-death differences persist in a biologically relevant way after death.

Now consider the genuinely intriguing possibility.

Perhaps certain trained practitioners approach dying with an unusually calm and physiologically distinctive state.

Perhaps their final illness and circumstances produce a particular configuration of bodily conditions.

Perhaps that configuration changes the rate at which decomposition becomes visible.

This is a plausible *class of hypotheses*.

But the causal chain remains unproven.

It would be extraordinary if decades of contemplative training were shown to influence postmortem decomposition in a reproducible way.

It would not automatically prove that consciousness survives death.

And there's a further possibility.

Maybe some prolonged preservation cases arise from combinations of ordinary factors that forensic science does not yet model well in these populations.

That would also be a valuable discovery.

**The interesting scientific question is not whether we can preserve the word “miracle.” It is whether we can explain the observations.**


## XIV. Coherence: Does the body remain organized because consciousness refuses to leave?

Now we arrive at the philosophical question that makes tukdam so fascinating.

What actually holds a living body together?

Not merely in the crude sense of why its tissues do not fall onto the floor.

But in the deeper sense of why billions of interacting cells maintain a coordinated, functional organism.

Cells constantly change.

Molecules are replaced.

Tissues repair themselves.

Immune responses adapt.

Physiological systems compensate for disturbances.

Your living body is not a static object.

**It is an actively maintained pattern.**

And maintaining that pattern requires ongoing work.

Energy.

Information processing.

Chemical gradients.

Electrical signalling.

Mechanical interactions.

Feedback.

Coordination.

In modern biology, life is not adequately explained by imagining that all the body's parts simply remain in place because they are physically attached.

Living organization involves continuous processes that resist certain forms of disorder.

Now death disrupts those processes.

Circulation ceases.

Oxygen delivery fails.

ATP production becomes insufficient.

Membrane gradients deteriorate.

Cellular regulatory systems progressively lose their capacity to maintain normal states.

The biological organization that was continually being maintained begins to disintegrate.

Different cells and tissues lose function at different rates.

And this brings us to a very important distinction.

### What does coherence actually mean?

The word *coherence* is thrown around with extraordinary recklessness in discussions of consciousness.

Sometimes it means synchronized neural oscillations.

Sometimes it means coordinated physiological activity.

Sometimes it means quantum coherence.

Sometimes it means that someone has had a particularly nice meditation and feels spiritually aligned with a tree.

These are not equivalent.

In neuroscience, coherence can refer to a defined statistical relationship between oscillatory signals.

In biology, coordination can refer to the regulated activities through which tissues and organs maintain function.

In thermodynamics, the maintenance of biological order involves energy-dependent, far-from-equilibrium processes.

And in quantum mechanics, coherence has a precise technical meaning related to phase relationships in quantum states.

You cannot simply use the same English word and assume you've discovered a single universal mechanism.

**A coherent sentence and a coherent quantum state do not become scientifically related because they're both called coherent.**

Nevertheless, the broader question remains legitimate.

Could the way an organism is coordinated before death influence how it decomposes afterward?

Yes, potentially.

But you would need to identify specific physiological variables, establish causal relationships and measure the relevant postmortem changes.

Could ongoing postmortem neural coherence explain tukdam?

The existing EEG findings do not support detectable persistent activity of the forms measured.

Could there be some other tissue-level biological process affecting decomposition?

Potentially.

But that process would need to be measured.

Could postmortem consciousness, as understood in the tantric tradition, explain preservation?

That remains a metaphysical hypothesis without independent empirical support.

So now let us introduce a scientist whose work provides one of the most interesting ways of *thinking* about biological organization.

Not because he has proved Tibetan rebirth.

He hasn't.

But because he has shown that biological form involves something far more interesting than a static genetic blueprint.


## XV. Michael Levin enters the chat, and the body turns out to be a deeply intelligent construction project.

You are dead.

Your body, in the fictional scenario, is exhibiting unusually delayed visible decomposition.

Meanwhile, in the world of living science, Michael Levin has spent years asking a remarkable question.

How does a collection of cells know what anatomical structure it is supposed to build?

Consider a developing embryo.

Cells divide.

Differentiate.

Move.

Respond to signals.

Construct tissues and organs.

But the outcome is not merely a random accumulation of local chemical reactions.

The result is a coordinated anatomical form.

An eye appears in a location appropriate for an eye.

A limb develops with a particular structure.

Organs are proportioned and positioned.

And, in regenerative organisms, lost structures can sometimes be restored.

Levin's work investigates the role of bioelectric signalling and distributed cellular intelligence in this organization.

The central point is that genetic information alone doesn't provide a complete description of how anatomical outcomes are dynamically regulated.

Cells communicate using ion channels, electrical potentials, gap junctions, biochemical signals and mechanical interactions.

Groups of cells can coordinate their behaviour toward large-scale anatomical outcomes.

Levin proposes that we can profitably study these systems using concepts from cognition and control theory.

Not because an individual skin cell possesses a tiny human intellect.

But because collective living systems can display goal-directed and error-correcting behaviour.

His 2025 *BioEssays* article, The Multiscale Wisdom of the Body, develops this approach to collective cellular intelligence and anatomical regulation.

And now for the extraordinary experiment.

### The two-headed flatworm that remembers a different body

Levin's collaborators study planarian flatworms.

These animals possess remarkable regenerative capacities.

Remove a head, and under suitable conditions the animal can regenerate one.

But now imagine temporarily perturbing the bioelectric signalling involved in regulating its anatomical polarity.

In a 2017 study, the researchers showed that changing endogenous bioelectric conditions could alter the pattern of regeneration.

Some animals regenerated with two heads.

That's already extraordinary.

But then came the truly fascinating observation.

The researchers cut the animals again.

Without repeating the original perturbation.

And altered regenerative outcomes could persist.

The anatomical patterning system had acquired a new stable configuration.

In the experiment, even animals whose external morphology looked ordinary could harbour a changed regenerative state that became apparent upon subsequent amputations.

This was not simply the genome acquiring a mutation specifying two heads.

The study attributed the altered target morphology to persistent changes in bioelectric patterning circuitry.

Now think about what that means.

The body isn't merely assembling itself by mechanically executing a fixed genetic script.

There are distributed regulatory processes that help determine what anatomical outcome the tissue will restore.

The system can remember, in a functional biological sense, a target pattern.

And that target can sometimes be altered.

**The body possesses forms of pattern memory that are not reducible to an unchanged DNA sequence.**

This is a remarkable empirical finding.

And now your philosophical mind begins racing.

### The anatomical setpoint

Imagine damaging a living structure.

The organism responds.

Cells proliferate.

Signals change.

Tissues reorganize.

And, in a regenerative organism, the system may move toward a particular anatomical outcome.

Levin describes such outcomes as target morphologies or anatomical setpoints.

The cells, collectively, navigate a space of possible anatomical configurations.

This isn't necessarily evidence that an individual cell consciously imagines a finished body.

It's an account of distributed regulation in which the system's behaviour can be described in terms of goals, memories and error correction.

And because these processes can be manipulated through bioelectrical interventions, they are scientifically tractable.

Change the relevant signals, and sometimes you change the structure the tissue builds.

This gives us a new way of understanding the living body.

Not merely as a collection of components.

But as a system continually maintaining and sometimes repairing organized form.

Now apply the question to death.

**What happens when the coordinated system loses the capacity to maintain its pattern?**

Ordinarily, the organization progressively deteriorates.

But perhaps the pattern of deterioration depends upon the organism's preceding state.

Perhaps some biological conditions preserve anatomical integrity longer than others.

That is a reasonable research question.

But we need to resist a very tempting leap.

The fact that bioelectric networks help living tissue maintain and regenerate form does not imply that an electrical pattern survives when its supporting tissue becomes irreversibly nonfunctional.

And the existence of anatomical pattern memory does not establish the survival of autobiographical consciousness.

A regenerating flatworm is not a deceased Tibetan meditator.

There is no direct experimental bridge from one phenomenon to the other.

But the research does challenge simplistic assumptions about where biological information is stored and how living organization is maintained.

And that makes it philosophically relevant.


## XVI. What if the pattern is the thinker, and the body is merely its writing surface?

Now we reach Levin's more speculative work.

And this is where things become genuinely exciting.

In November 2024, Levin published an essay called Who's the Data? Implications of the Thoughts-Are-Thinkers Continuum for Developmental Bioelectricity.

The title is asking a wonderfully strange question.

We usually think of a person as the thinker and thoughts as things produced by the person.

But what if that distinction is not as absolute as we suppose?

What if sufficiently persistent, organized patterns can themselves be analysed as agents?

In the essay, Levin explores a conceptual reversal.

Rather than thinking of the physical body as the agent and bioelectric patterns as its data, perhaps we can investigate the patterns themselves as agents, with the tissue acting as a medium through which they read and write information.

This is explicitly exploratory theorizing.

Not a demonstrated claim that electrical patterns are independent conscious souls.

But it is a fascinating inversion of the ordinary explanatory relationship.

Now imagine you are a pattern.

Not a frozen geometrical design.

A self-maintaining dynamic organization.

You persist through changes in material.

Your physical components are replaced.

Your structure is continually reconstituted through interactions with the environment.

What makes you the same pattern over time?

Not the numerical identity of every molecule.

It's some form of causal and organizational continuity.

Now consider a whirlpool.

The water flowing through it continually changes.

Yet the vortex may persist.

It isn't a fixed object made from the same water molecules.

It's a dynamic pattern maintained by ongoing physical conditions.

Or consider Levin's discussion of a glider in Conway's Game of Life.

The glider is not a solid object travelling through a material medium.

It is a recurring pattern of local state changes.

The configuration propagates through the system.

Its persistence depends upon the rules and substrate permitting those successive states.

This is a powerful analogy for understanding how a pattern can persist while its immediate material implementation changes.

And now you see why such ideas might interest someone investigating Buddhist consciousness.

Buddhism rejects a permanent, independently existing self.

Instead, it describes a causally continuous succession of conditioned phenomena.

Levin's work explores ways that identity, agency and memory may be understood at the level of distributed patterns.

There's a genuine philosophical resonance.

### But here is the enormous missing step.

A pattern can persist through changes in its substrate only when there is some mechanism supporting that persistence.

The whirlpool requires fluid dynamics.

The glider requires successive states of the cellular automaton.

The living bioelectric network requires functioning cells, electrochemical gradients and an environment supporting their operation.

So if the human organism dies, and the systems supporting its neural and bodily organization irreversibly break down, **what carries the relevant conscious continuity onward?**

That is the problem.

If someone proposes that consciousness transfers into a different biological substrate, how does the transfer occur?

What information is preserved?

What carries the causal influence?

How does it connect with a future organism?

Why would it preserve the karmic dispositions described in Buddhist texts rather than merely anatomical patterns?

And how could we test whether such a process had occurred?

Levin's biology does not presently supply those answers.

It opens up interesting questions about distributed agency, memory and the organization of living systems.

It does not demonstrate reincarnation.

Nor does it establish an immaterial mental continuum or the existence of Buddha-fields.

### The difference between persistence of form and persistence of subjectivity

This distinction is indispensable.

An anatomical pattern can remain recognizable after the life processes maintaining it have ceased.

A photograph can preserve information about a person.

A written record can preserve their words.

A computer can simulate aspects of their behaviour.

But none of these facts, by themselves, establishes that the person's conscious experience continues.

Now imagine an unusually preserved corpse.

Its face remains recognizable.

Its tissue structure changes slowly.

We might discover an unexpected biochemical explanation.

Or a previously underappreciated regulatory process influencing postmortem decay.

But we would still need a separate account of subjective awareness.

**The persistence of information is not automatically the persistence of the experiencer.**

And the question becomes even more difficult because Buddhism itself does not posit an intrinsically existing experiencer.

It describes a conditioned continuum without a permanent owner.

So a serious comparison must distinguish three things.

The continuity of an organized biological pattern.

The continuity of cognitive or informational functions.

And the continuity of subjective awareness.

They may be related.

But no established scientific theory allows us simply to identify them.

Now your fictional practitioner is dead.

And his body's appearance is unusual.

Perhaps there is some biological persistence of structure.

Perhaps there are unusual environmental conditions.

Perhaps the community is interpreting a real but incompletely understood pattern of decomposition.

Or perhaps the traditional explanation captures something that present science cannot yet test.

We cannot decide between these possibilities merely by invoking coherence.

The evidence must do the work.

And we should not pretend that a speculative analogy has already solved the mystery.


## XVII. What would a serious scientific explanation of tukdam actually require?

Now imagine bringing the researchers, Tibetan physicians, tantric scholars and developmental biologists into one room.

An extraordinary gathering.

The Tibetan scholars bring centuries of phenomenological and ritual knowledge.

The neuroscientists bring EEG equipment.

The forensic investigators bring instruments for studying decomposition.

The microbiologists bring sampling methods.

The developmental biologists bring models of tissue coordination.

And the philosopher brings seventeen questions about consciousness which everyone immediately regrets inviting him to ask.

The first challenge is to agree upon the thing being investigated.

Are we trying to determine whether postmortem EEG activity persists?

Are we investigating unusual tissue preservation?

Are we asking whether meditation changes physiological conditions before death?

Or are we testing a theory of consciousness continuing beyond irreversible brain function?

These require different experiments.

### The preservation question

A serious study would ideally follow practitioners prospectively, with appropriate consent.

It would characterize their medical history, age, diet, medication use, illnesses, body composition and meditation background.

It would measure relevant physiological variables during life and, when practicable, around the dying process.

Postmortem investigation would require standardized measurements of temperature, humidity, surface changes, and the microbial and chemical processes of decay.

And it would need suitable comparison groups.

Not simply another corpse located in a different climate and preserved under different conditions.

Otherwise, an apparently striking difference could be caused by uncontrolled environmental or biological factors.

The ethical requirements are substantial.

Tibetan communities may regard the body as being in an especially sensitive transitional condition.

Scientific investigators must respect consent and cultural restrictions.

But those restrictions also limit which measurements can be made.

The 2024 forensic paper acknowledged that internal temperature measurements and turning the body to examine certain surfaces were not permitted in the investigated cases.

Researchers also lacked observations from the earliest postmortem days and did not always have complete medical histories.

These limitations do not make the findings worthless.

They determine what conclusions the findings can responsibly support.

### The coherence question

If we hypothesize that some form of tissue-level coordination persists after the cessation of ordinary circulation, we must define what is being coordinated.

Electrical potentials?

Ion gradients?

Metabolic activity?

Gene expression?

Membrane integrity?

Inflammatory responses?

We would then need to measure those processes.

And if the proposed mechanism requires metabolic energy, we must explain where that energy comes from after circulation and oxygen delivery have ceased.

The fact that some molecules, transcripts and individual cellular processes persist for a time after organismal death does not demonstrate that integrated organism-level consciousness remains.

An active, self-maintaining biological system is not the same thing as a progressively degrading system that still contains residual chemical activity.

### The consciousness question

This is the hardest one.

The Tibetan religious account proposes that an extremely subtle consciousness can remain associated with the body after the cessation of gross physiological functions.

But how would we independently detect that consciousness?

If ordinary neural activity is absent, conventional EEG may be unable to address the claim directly.

However, declaring the consciousness undetectable does not automatically establish its existence.

A serious explanatory model must generate some distinguishable consequences.

Otherwise the hypothesis remains metaphysical.

And metaphysical theories can be investigated philosophically, but they should not be misrepresented as experimentally verified.

Here is where the most intellectually productive conversation would occur.

The Tibetan tradition can clarify what it actually predicts.

The scientists can clarify which predictions are measurable.

And both can examine whether any unexpected observations demand new explanations.

That is vastly more interesting than either pretending the mystery has been solved or refusing to investigate it.

Now, with all that established, let's return to our practitioner.

Because the fourth death is not over.

And the story is about to become rather unsettling.


## XVIII. You've been dead for three days. Your meditation teacher is still talking to you.

Your body rests in a quiet room.

In this fictional fourth death, the community has decided to observe the customary period of non-disturbance.

There are no theatrical displays.

No miraculous beams of light shooting through the ceiling.

No levitating furniture.

Your companions simply maintain the environment with care.

Someone recites an aspiration prayer.

Someone remembers your kindness.

Someone quietly reflects upon the impermanence of the body.

And now we return to the internal account, which is not something the observers can independently verify.

According to the Tibetan interpretation of tukdam, the subtle consciousness may remain absorbed in a meditative condition after gross physiological functions have ceased.

The body is no longer alive in the ordinary biomedical sense.

But the traditional account proposes that the extremely subtle mind has not yet completed the death transition.

The practitioner may be resting in the clear light.

Now here's a critical complication.

**Remaining in a subtle state does not necessarily mean attaining final liberation.**

Some traditional accounts distinguish the capacity to remain in the death luminosity from the realization required to transform it into fully liberating wisdom.

A person might possess considerable contemplative stability without having completely eradicated ignorance.

And a person might possess profound realization without displaying the longest period of outward bodily preservation.

The duration of apparent tukdam is not therefore a simple enlightenment score.

You cannot look at a body and say:

*Seven days. Excellent. Probably fourth bhūmi.*

*Fourteen days. Very impressive.*

*Thirty-eight days. Absolutely guaranteed Buddhahood.*

That would be an extraordinarily silly way to evaluate realization.

Some Tibetan accounts treat tukdam as a sign of significant accomplishment.

But outward signs and spiritual status are not mathematically interchangeable.

### Meanwhile, the distinction between mind and body becomes unbearable.

Here lies the body.

A material organism.

No ordinary respiration.

No functioning circulatory system.

No recognizable EEG signals, if our fictional case resembled the negative findings of the published postmortem study.

And the religious tradition describes a subtle consciousness remaining in meditation.

If that description is true, then ordinary brain-dependent consciousness is not the whole story.

But if the description is mistaken, then perhaps the preserved appearance is simply an unusual biological outcome.

And the observers cannot determine the truth of the consciousness claim merely by examining the face.

This is why tukdam is not merely a curiosity about decomposition.

It forces an unusually sharp confrontation between different understandings of death.

For biomedicine, the clinically relevant question concerns irreversible loss of the functions defining death under accepted criteria.

For Tibetan tantric philosophy, the final departure of subtle consciousness may define a further transition.

The same physical body is being interpreted within two different explanatory systems.

And those systems do not automatically agree about what counts as the final event.

Donagh Coleman's 2025 anthropological study examines precisely this issue: how different ways of conceiving the body and death shape the observation and interpretation of tukdam.

Now imagine that the community eventually observes changes traditionally associated with the end of tukdam.

The body's appearance begins to alter more rapidly.

The posture changes.

Signs of decomposition become more pronounced.

The community understands that the subtle meditative period has ended.

Does this prove that consciousness has just departed?

No.

It is the traditional interpretation of the observed changes.

But within our story, let us follow what that interpretation would imply.

The meditative state concludes.

The intermediate-state process may proceed.

And now, for the fourth time, the great question returns.

**Have you actually recognized the nature of mind?**


## XIX. Four ways your fourth death could go

You have become a serious practitioner.

Not a mere collector of sacred books.

Not a person whose entire spiritual achievement consists of having an excellent opinion about emptiness.

You've trained in meditation.

You've investigated awareness.

You've cultivated compassion.

You've become familiar with the dissolution of mental activity.

So your possible outcomes are different from those in your first three lives.

But they still diverge substantially.

### Ending A: You recognize the clear light.

The subtlest mind manifests.

The nature of experience is recognized directly.

There is no grasping at a separate experiencer.

In the relevant tantric account, realization of emptiness and luminosity is brought fully into the death process.

The transition becomes an opportunity for liberation.

This isn't the personal ego achieving immortality.

The compulsive cycle of ignorance and karmic becoming is interrupted.

From the Dzogchen perspective, the recognition concerns rigpa and the primordial nature of awareness, although its precise doctrinal relationship to the Geluk completion-stage presentation requires careful distinction.

If you achieve definitive liberation here, our story's fifth involuntary death never occurs.

Congratulations.

You've finally ended the series.

Unfortunately, the author has another chapter planned.

So we must investigate other possibilities.

### Ending B: You cannot sustain recognition, but phowa is successful.

You have practised an appropriate transference method under a qualified teacher.

At death, the necessary causes and conditions come together.

Within that tradition's account, consciousness is directed toward Amitābha's Sukhāvatī or another favourable destination.

You have not necessarily achieved the highest realization at the moment of death.

But you have obtained conditions for completing the path.

This is a different outcome from the direct recognition of dharmakāya.

The traditional practice may call it liberation through transference, but the precise meaning depends on the level and destination involved.

Again, if this succeeds definitively, the fifth ordinary rebirth might not be necessary.

And our narrative becomes embarrassingly short.

### Ending C: You remain in tukdam and then enter the bardo.

This is the possibility we're going to follow.

You possess considerable meditative stability.

Within the religious narrative, you remain absorbed in an extremely subtle condition after the cessation of gross physiological functions.

Your body exhibits unusually slow visible decomposition.

But the relevant recognition is not fully stabilized.

The clear-light experience eventually ceases.

The subsequent appearances unfold.

And karmic continuity proceeds.

Now your lifelong practice becomes important.

You're familiar with the teachings.

You have trained with dreams.

You have learned not to take every appearance as an independently existing threat.

The bardo of dharmatā may therefore provide further opportunities.

You encounter luminous manifestations.

But this time, you are not simply running away.

The peaceful appearances are not automatically classified as enemies.

The wrathful appearances do not necessarily provoke complete terror.

You may recognize something of their empty nature.

The capacity has grown.

But a deeply subtle form of grasping remains.

And here's a devastating possibility.

**You have become attached to being a meditator.**

Not merely attached to possessions.

Not merely attached to personal relationships.

You are attached to the very identity of someone who has spent decades seeking liberation.

You want to be the one who understands.

The one who recognizes.

The one who achieves.

And, according to the deeper Buddhist critique, that is still a form of ignorance.

Even the idea of liberation can become an object of grasping.

You have spent your life building an exquisitely refined spiritual identity.

Now death removes the organism that supported it.

And the remaining question is whether the identification itself has been relinquished.

You approach recognition.

But you still subtly establish a separate subject who hopes to possess the experience.

The opportunity slips away.

The intermediate-state continuum continues.

And another rebirth becomes possible.

### Ending D: You die suddenly and cannot use the training as expected.

We should not overlook this one.

Not everyone dies gradually.

And not every death proceeds through a prolonged, peaceful bedside experience.

Accidents happen.

Illness can alter consciousness.

The brain can be injured.

Anesthesia, delirium, severe pain and many other conditions can affect ordinary awareness.

Traditional Tibetan teachings provide various methods for different circumstances, but none of this should be turned into the claim that a sudden or confused death is evidence of poor spiritual accomplishment.

The relevant Buddhist systems attribute the ultimate course to complex causes and conditions, not simply to a practitioner's performance in the final observable seconds.

This is one reason regular practice matters.

It aims to develop dispositions that do not depend entirely upon an ideal deathbed environment.

But no practitioner can guarantee their dying circumstances.

And compassionate care should never be sacrificed to some imagined ideal of spiritual heroism.


## XX. You're alive!

Oh, for fuck's sake.

Again.

For the fifth time, the process has produced a human life.

At least our fourth incarnation was a considerable improvement.

You spent decades practising seriously.

You became familiar with subtle states.

You investigated the nature of mind.

Your death was treated by your community as a significant meditative event.

And, in the fictional narrative, you passed through the intermediate state with much less confusion than before.

But a final subtle identification remained.

So the continuum of conditioned becoming continued.

Now, this fifth life begins under favourable circumstances.

You're born in a place where the teachings of the Great Perfection are available.

You eventually encounter a teacher who knows the Nyingma Dzogchen tradition.

And you discover that, although you've been studying Tibetan death yoga for a very long time, the final teaching may require a fundamental change in approach.

Because the ultimate question is no longer how to control the winds.

Or how to maintain an extraordinary state.

Or how to direct consciousness toward a better destination.

Or even how to remain in meditation after the body has ceased functioning.

The question is whether the nature of awareness can be recognized **without fabricating another state to possess**.

Now we approach *trekchö*.

The cutting through of primordial ignorance.

And *tögal*.

The extraordinary Dzogchen visionary practices associated with the spontaneous display of luminosity.

And then the most outrageous claim in this entire five-death trilogy-that-became-five-chapters.

**Rainbow body.**

Not the body acquiring a political affiliation.

Not someone becoming exceptionally colourful at a spiritual festival.

Traditional Dzogchen literature describes extraordinary modes of realization associated, in some accounts, with the dissolution or transformation of the physical body.

Other traditions describe the *great transference*.

And the philosophical stakes are enormous.

Because if ordinary death yoga investigates whether consciousness persists when the body's functions cease, the most radical rainbow-body claims appear to concern the relationship between realization and the material appearance of the body itself.

Now, the actual reports vary.

Their textual histories vary.

Their evidential quality varies.

And none of them has established a scientifically verified process by which a human body transforms into light.

But within Dzogchen, these teachings are not merely an ornamental afterthought.

They are connected with its account of primordial purity, spontaneous presence, the nature of appearances, and the realization of the three kāyas.

To understand why the tradition makes such extraordinary claims, we need to examine its metaphysics rather than simply stare at the miracle stories.

And that is the final chapter.


## XXI. The final philosophical problem: What exactly dies?

Now, before we finish, consider how far we've come.

In the first chapter, death appeared to be the end of a particular embodied life followed by karmically conditioned continuation.

In the second, the great danger was mistaking luminous appearances for independent objects of attraction or fear.

In the third, we investigated whether compassion, merit and aspiration could change the conditions governing that continuation.

And now, in the fourth, we've examined the possibility that the very distinction between body and consciousness may be more complicated than ordinary assumptions suggest.

But there is an important danger.

You can become so fascinated by extraordinary death phenomena that you forget the central Buddhist problem.

You want to know whether consciousness survives death.

You want to know whether tukdam is real.

You want to know whether some hidden coherence sustains the organism.

You want to know whether bioelectric patterns possess a kind of distributed agency.

These are serious questions.

But suppose you answered every single one.

Suppose science established that some form of consciousness continued after biological death.

Would you thereby be liberated?

No.

You might simply discover that the capacity for confusion survives longer than you imagined.

**An immortal delusion is still a delusion.**

Now imagine the opposite.

Suppose death is ultimately the irreversible cessation of individual experience.

Would that mean compassion was meaningless?

Would the cultivation of wisdom suddenly become irrelevant?

Would the suffering you cause others become unimportant?

No.

The ethical and contemplative questions remain profound even without the empirical verification of rebirth.

But within Tibetan Buddhism, the claims about rebirth and liberation form a coherent and far-reaching metaphysical system.

The possibility of continuity across death changes the stakes of practice.

Every action may contribute to future existence.

Every act of grasping may reinforce the conditions for continued saṃsāra.

Every moment of recognition may weaken those conditions.

And the culmination of the path is not a particularly impressive death.

It is awakening.

Now return to our great recurring phrase.

**You are dead.**

What does that actually mean?

Biologically, the present organism has irreversibly ceased functioning.

Conventionally, the person whose life others knew has died.

Within the Tibetan tantric framework, the subtle mental continuum may continue through the death process.

Within Dzogchen, the deepest insight concerns the empty, luminous nature of awareness that was never an independently existing personal possession.

And this is why the traditions refuse to identify liberation with simply acquiring a new destination.

The problem is not merely that you have been living in the wrong place.

You have been *mistaking what you are*.

Again.

And again.

And again.

You've attempted to solve the problem by becoming wealthy.

By becoming morally respectable.

By becoming intellectually sophisticated.

By becoming compassionate.

By becoming an accomplished meditator.

Each of these can change your life.

Some provide indispensable conditions for the path.

But none automatically destroys the basic ignorance through which an independent self is imagined.

And now, in the fifth life, you're approaching the most radical of all the Tibetan teachings.

Not a technique for making another experience happen.

Not a strategy for escaping to a safer location.

Not a method of improving the spiritual biography of a separate person.

But the direct recognition of the nature of the entire process.

You have spent beginningless lifetimes asking:

*What will happen to me when I die?*

And the final teaching is going to ask:

**Who the fuck is this “me” that you keep insisting has been travelling?**

Not to deny the conventional continuum.

Not to dismiss karma.

Not to replace the Buddhist path with a fashionable slogan about being one with the universe.

But to investigate, relentlessly, whether the person you imagine yourself to be has ever existed in the way you've assumed.

Now the fifth death approaches.

And this time, if the Dzogchen account is true and its recognition is fully realized, the ending may be radically different.

Not because you have finally discovered how to survive death forever as the same individual.

But because there may no longer be the ignorance that makes involuntary rebirth necessary.

**For four deaths, you have been trying to escape the prison.**

In the fifth, you may finally recognize how the prison was being constructed.

And why, in the deepest sense, the door was never locked by anything existing independently of the mistake itself.
