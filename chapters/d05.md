# Chapter V

## You're Dead. Except There Is No Longer Anyone to Die.

*The Great Perfection. Samantabhadra. The primordial ground. The four visions. Rainbow body. The last bardo. And the frankly outrageous possibility that the entire machinery of rebirth has been running on a misunderstanding.*

*Our final chapter draws especially on Longchenpa's* Essential Summary *and* Great Guide to the Supreme Secret Path; *Jigme Lingpa's* Prayer of the Ground, Path and Fruition *and* Aspiration for the Bardos; *Patrul Rinpoche's* Special Teaching of the Wise and Glorious King; *Dodrupchen Jigme Tenpé Nyima's* Answers on Dzogchen; *and Karma Lingpa's* Root Verses on the Six Bardos. *The dialogue and five-life narrative are invented, not scriptural quotations.*


## I. You are alive\!

Again.

**FOR FUCK'S SAKE.**

Five lives.

Five entire bloody human incarnations.

Not counting the little misunderstanding involving hell.

You have now spent an extraordinary amount of time attempting to solve a problem you have still not fully understood.

Let's review.

**Life One\:** You were a vicious bastard.

You died.

You encountered the opportunity for liberation.

You missed it.

You experienced the consequences of destructive karma and had an extremely unpleasant encounter with the hell realms.

**Life Two\:** You became a Buddhist intellectual.

You could explain emptiness.

You could identify the Buddha families.

You could probably have delivered an outstanding lecture on the peaceful and wrathful deities.

Then you died.

Forty-two peaceful Buddhas appeared.

You ran away.

Fifty-eight wrathful deities appeared.

You ran away even faster.

You experienced the karmic tribunal, attempted to negotiate with the Lord of Death, and eventually returned to earthly existence.

**Life Three\:** You developed compassion.

You fed geckos.

You recited mantras to small reptiles who thought you were having a nervous breakdown.

You cultivated merit, bodhicitta and aspiration toward Amitābha's Sukhāvatī.

You died.

Your disposition was considerably more wholesome.

But you still hadn't fully realized the nature of mind.

**Life Four\:** You actually meditated.

For decades.

Dream yoga.

Luminosity.

Subtle winds.

The dissolution of the elements.

The mother and child clear lights.

You understood phowa.

You practised seriously.

You even died in a manner your companions interpreted as *tukdam*\.

Scientists arrived with electrodes.

The body behaved unusually.

The monks discussed the subtle mind.

The neuroscientists discussed the absence of measurable EEG signals.

The forensic investigators discussed bacteria.

Someone brought up Michael Levin.

And somehow, after all this, your karmic continuity still entered another human existence.

A particularly accomplished spiritual career.

But not quite liberation.

And now-

You are alive.

Again.

But this time you're going to encounter a teaching so radical that it threatens to undermine the very notion of a separate practitioner progressing toward enlightenment.

Not by denying the path.

Not by saying ethics and meditation are pointless.

But by asking whether the ultimate nature of awareness was ever something that needed to be manufactured.

You are about to encounter Dzogchen.

The Great Perfection.

And its first great claim is almost offensively simple.

**You are trying to produce something that, at the level of the ground, was never absent.**

Oh, that's just wonderful.

You've spent five lifetimes attempting to attain enlightenment, and someone is about to suggest that you've been approaching the entire enterprise like a man desperately searching for the spectacles already sitting on his nose.


## II. You meet a Dzogchen master, and he immediately ruins your spiritual career.

You're twenty-nine.

You've spent years studying the Buddhist traditions.

You appreciate the Madhyamaka critique of inherent existence.

You're fascinated by the subtle mind described in highest yoga tantra.

You understand the doctrine of dependent origination.

You're particularly interested in the relationship between luminosity, emptiness and the continuity of consciousness.

You've developed a serious meditation practice.

You can remain relatively undistracted for long periods.

Sometimes extraordinary states arise.

Clarity.

Stillness.

Bliss.

The impression that ordinary subject-object distinctions have weakened.

And you are extremely pleased with yourself.

You sit before a Dzogchen teacher.

He asks what you are seeking.

You explain that you wish to attain recognition of the primordial nature of mind.

He asks\:

*What is it that you think you're going to attain?*

You explain the inseparability of emptiness and luminosity.

You offer a detailed account of the two truths.

You mention that your previous understanding of consciousness was influenced by the subtle-body accounts of the completion stage.

He listens.

Then asks\:

*And who is the one who hopes to possess this realization?*

Oh, for fuck's sake.

Not again.

You explain that the self is conventionally designated upon the aggregates.

He asks whether you're currently examining that fact or simply repeating a theory.

You begin to describe the difference between conceptual and nonconceptual cognition.

He waits.

You realize, with growing irritation, that you've been caught.

You're still trying to obtain a special experience.

You've simply replaced the desire for money, sex, status and recognition with the desire to become someone who has realized the ultimate nature of reality.

A wonderfully spiritual ambition.

Still an ambition.

You want to become the owner of enlightenment.

**You want the ego to survive long enough to receive an official certificate stating that the ego never existed.**

This is going to be a problem.

Now, a proper Dzogchen teacher does not simply hand every unprepared person an improvised pointing-out instruction.

Actual *ngo sprod*, direct introduction, belongs to a lineage context, with preparation, guidance and verification of understanding.

Our fictional dialogue is an illustration, not a substitute for that transmission.

But it conveys the central issue identified by Patrul Rinpoche in his famous presentation of Garab Dorje's three statements.

The first essential point is direct recognition of rigpa.

The second is decisive confidence in that recognition.

The third is confidence in the liberation of whatever arises.

Patrul Rinpoche explicitly warns against mistaking pleasant or subtle meditative experiences for realization. 

You ask\:

*So what am I supposed to do?*

Your teacher replies\:

*First understand what Dzogchen is actually claiming.*

At last.

Something to study.

You reach for your notebook.

He sighs.


## III. The primordial ground: Before the universe, before you, before the fucking problem.

The Tibetan term *rdzogs pa chen po* means Great Perfection or Great Completeness.

And the most radical part of the teaching concerns the **ground**\.

Tibetan:

Wylie: *gzhi*\.

In Dzogchen, the ground is the fundamental nature associated with both saṃsāra and nirvāṇa.

But immediately we must eliminate several misunderstandings.

The ground is not a creator God.

It is not a cosmic person.

It is not necessarily a universal substance out of which individual souls are manufactured.

Nor is it an enormous immortal consciousness sitting behind the universe watching everything happen.

The tradition specifically resists treating the ground as a self-established object.

The ground is described through three inseparable aspects\:

| **Essence**<br><br>ngo bo | Primordial purity, *ka dag*\: empty, unoriginated, never intrinsically stained. |
|--------|--------|
| **Nature**<br><br>rang bzhin | Spontaneous presence, *lhun grub*\: luminous, expressive, the source of manifestation. |
| **Compassionate responsiveness**<br><br>thugs rje | Unobstructed responsiveness or expressive capacity, not merely an emotional feeling of kindness. |

These aren't three separate ingredients combined to manufacture consciousness.

They're distinguishable ways of describing a reality whose empty essence and luminous expression are inseparable.

Longchenpa's *Essential Summary* explicitly links the threefold ground with the possibility of either recognizing appearances as its manifestation or becoming confused by them.

Now let's take each aspect seriously.

### Ka dag: primordial purity

Tibetan:

Wylie: *ka dag*\.

The first syllable, *ka*, is used in this expression to evoke what is primordial or original.

*Dag* means pure.

But primordial purity does not mean that there was once a lovely clean universe which subsequently became dirty.

It means that, at the level of ultimate nature, phenomena were never established as independently existing, intrinsically defiled entities.

This is subtle.

Suppose you experience anger.

The anger is genuinely occurring as a conditioned psychological event.

It may have painful consequences.

You may act destructively because of it.

Dzogchen doesn't say\:

*Congratulations, anger is primordial purity, go and punch somebody.*

That would be spiritual idiocy.

Rather, the teaching concerns the nature of the anger.

Does it exist as an independently established thing?

Can it be found as a fixed entity, separate from the conditions through which it arises?

Is its apparent solidity intrinsic?

Or is it an empty, dependently arisen appearance?

Primordial purity describes the absence of inherent defilement at the level of nature.

It does not deny the conventional causal reality of ignorance and suffering.

**The ground is not dirty because beings are confused about it.**

Just as misunderstanding a mathematical relationship doesn't alter the relationship itself.

Or mistaking a rope for a snake doesn't turn the rope into an actual cobra.

Although, while the misunderstanding lasts, you can experience very real terror.

### Lhun grub: spontaneous presence

Tibetan:

Wylie: *lhun grub*\.

Now we reach the other side of the teaching.

If the ground is empty, why does anything appear?

Why isn't everything an absolute blank?

Why are there colours?

Thoughts?

Perceptions?

Buddhas?

Terrifying wrathful deities?

Geckos?

Why has reality apparently invested so much effort in producing little animals capable of running across ceilings?

Dzogchen refuses to identify emptiness with mere nothingness.

Its account of spontaneous presence concerns the luminous expressive aspect of the ground.

Appearances can arise without possessing inherent existence.

The empty nature does not obstruct manifestation.

And manifestation does not contradict emptiness.

Think of a rainbow.

It appears.

You can perceive its colours.

You can photograph it.

You can identify the optical conditions giving rise to it.

Yet there is no solid, self-existing multicoloured object hanging in the sky.

Appearance and absence of inherent solidity are compatible.

Now extend that insight beyond the particular example.

An appearance need not be an inherently existing entity to appear.

The tradition's language of spontaneous presence emphasizes that the possibility of manifestation is not something added externally to an otherwise inert absolute.

But don't turn this into a crude claim that physics has discovered a self-aware cosmic light field.

**The term luminosity is not simply a reference to electromagnetic radiation.**

Its contemplative meaning cannot be reduced to visible photons.

### Thugs rje: compassionate responsiveness

Now the third aspect.

*Thugs rje* is often translated as compassion.

But in Dzogchen descriptions of the ground, it also conveys an unobstructed capacity for manifestation or responsiveness.

This is important because otherwise we might imagine that the ground has a human personality and simply feels nice about everyone.

That's not the intended meaning.

The ground's empty and luminous character is associated with an expressive openness capable of manifesting in relation to beings.

At the level of awakened fruition, this is connected with the spontaneous activity of Buddhas for the welfare of others.

This gives us the threefold logic.

Empty essence.

Luminous nature.

Unobstructed responsiveness.

And none exists separately from the others.

The ground is not a substance first created by emptiness, subsequently illuminated by consciousness and finally persuaded to become compassionate.

The three are inseparable descriptions.

You sit in astonishment.

Then ask the obvious question.

*Right. If everything is primordially pure, why the fuck have I spent five lifetimes being miserable?*

Excellent.

We have reached the central metaphysical mystery.


## IV. Samantabhadra: The Buddha who never fell for the bullshit.

Your teacher introduces Samantabhadra\.

In Tibetan, *Kuntuzangpo*\.

The All-Good.

Traditionally represented as a blue, unadorned Buddha, sometimes united with Samantabhadrī.

But the important distinction is that this is the primordial Buddha of Nyingma Dzogchen, not simply the similarly named bodhisattva Samantabhadra of the broader Mahāyāna tradition.



Now, what does Samantabhadra represent?

Not a historical monarch of consciousness.

Not a deity who manufactured the cosmos.

Not the first little individual who happened to become enlightened before everyone else.

Within the Dzogchen presentation, Samantabhadra personifies primordial awakening: the recognition of the ground without the arising of delusion.

The classic Prayer of Kuntuzangpo, revealed in the Northern Treasures tradition of Rigdzin Gödem, presents the whole drama through one fundamental principle.

One ground.

Two possibilities of recognition.

Two radically different outcomes.

From the same fundamental ground, recognition is associated with awakening.

Failure to recognize gives rise to the processes of saṃsāra.

The distinction is not between two separate ultimate substances.

It's between knowing and misrecognizing.

Now imagine an appearance arising.

Something luminous.

Immediately present.

Empty of intrinsic essence.

What happens?

In the awakened mode, it is recognized without being grasped as an independently existing object.

There is no need for a separate self to possess or defend itself against that appearance.

In the deluded mode, recognition fails.

The appearance becomes something over there.

An experiencer becomes someone over here.

Attraction and fear begin organizing the relationship.

And out of this fundamental dualistic misapprehension, the entire structure of saṃsāra develops.

The *Prayer of Kuntuzangpo* describes a progression from unawareness and bewilderment to anxiety, the differentiation of self and other, reinforced habitual tendencies, and the proliferation of the five afflictive poisons.

Do you see how devastating this is?

The problem is not that consciousness was originally a pure soul and then committed some primordial moral crime.

Nor is it that a cosmic God decided to punish it by creating a universe.

It is a problem of nonrecognition.

**Experience arises, and the very nature of the arising is not recognized.**

From that failure, the apparent distinction between subject and object becomes reified.

Then comes grasping.

Then aversion.

Then karmic action.

Then habitual conditioning.

Then the six realms.

Then birth.

Ageing.

Death.

Again.

And again.

And again.

You've spent five lives investigating the consequences.

Dzogchen is asking you to examine the original mistake.

### But why did ignorance arise in the first place?

Now here comes the question everyone should be asking.

If the ground is primordially pure, how can ignorance arise from it?

If ignorance is part of the ground, the ground doesn't seem perfectly pure.

If ignorance arises independently, the ground doesn't seem fundamental.

And if the ground is timeless, how can we speak of a first moment when delusion began?

This is not a trivial objection.

Traditional Dzogchen thinkers have confronted exactly this problem.

Dodrupchen Jigme Tenpé Nyima discusses whether the ground of delusion and ground of liberation can really be identified, and explains that the fundamental clear light itself is not intrinsically corrupted by delusion.

Instead, confusion concerns the failure to recognize the ground and grasping at its manifestations.

He also emphasizes the difference between ground-level luminosity that remains unrecognized and the irreversible realization of fruition.

Now here's the point that saves us from constructing an impossible cosmic origin story.

Buddhism generally does not posit a first historical moment of saṃsāra.

Beginningless ignorance is not the claim that there was a specific Tuesday on which awareness became stupid.

It is the claim that no first temporal beginning of the conditioned cycle can be established.

Dzogchen's primordial ground is not simply an earlier event in universal history.

Consequently, asking what happened *before* ignorance can mistakenly impose a temporal sequence upon what the teaching presents as more fundamental than that sequence.

Does this completely resolve every philosophical difficulty?

No.

The precise relationship between the ground, its manifestations and delusion remains a subject of sophisticated interpretation.

But it prevents the most obvious misunderstanding.

**The ground is not a cosmic substance that accidentally became ignorant and is now desperately attempting to repair itself.**

The ignorance belongs to the deluded mode of apprehending appearances.

Not to an independently existing stain permanently lodged inside ultimate reality.

You lean back.

*So Samantabhadra is the one who never made the original mistake?*

In the personified account, yes.

*And I'm the one who has been making it for beginningless time?*

Conventionally, your continuum is caught in ignorance.

*And the difference is recognition?*

At the most fundamental level of this presentation, yes.

*Jesus Christ.*

Quite.


## V. Rigpa versus sem: You've spent your entire life mistaking the receptionist for the CEO.

The next distinction may be the most important in the entire Great Perfection.

*Sem*\.

Tibetan:

Ordinary mind.

And *rigpa*\.

Tibetan:

Awareness, knowing, or pristine awareness in its specialized Dzogchen sense.

But immediately, we must be careful.

Ordinary mind is not some intrinsically evil substance.

Nor is rigpa an immortal little witness hiding behind ordinary mental activity.

The distinction concerns deluded cognition and the recognition of awareness's nature.

### Ordinary mind: sem

Suppose you're sitting quietly.

A sound occurs.

You recognize it.

A memory arises.

You remember yesterday.

A desire arises.

You imagine tomorrow.

A thought appears.

You start evaluating it.

And almost instantly, you have a complete narrative.

*I am a particular person.*

*I have a particular history.*

*Something is happening to me.*

*I like this.*

*I dislike that.*

*I must do something about it.*

Ordinary consciousness functions through distinctions, memory, interpretation and identification.

These are useful at the conventional level.

You need to recognize the difference between a poisonous mushroom and a perfectly acceptable dinner.

You need to remember where you live.

You need to distinguish your bank account from somebody else's.

Buddhist awakening doesn't require becoming a cognitively incapacitated vegetable.

The problem is not conventional differentiation.

It is the reification accompanying ignorance.

You take the person constructed through conditioned processes and experience them as a self-existing owner of those processes.

It's as though your brain has appointed a managing director who is, in fact, an elaborate fictional character.

Then the managing director begins demanding executive compensation.

### Rigpa: the recognition you've been missing

Rigpa is not merely the ordinary mind becoming unusually quiet.

Nor is it a thought that says\:

*I am aware.*

That is another thought.

Nor is rigpa simply the act of observing thought from a distance.

Because that can re-establish a separate observer.

And it isn't the blank absence of mental content.

You can sit in an extremely peaceful, unthinking state and remain ignorant of its nature.

The specialized Dzogchen term refers to recognition of the knowing, empty nature of awareness, beyond habitual dualistic grasping.

The word itself has a broad range in Tibetan, so we should not pretend that every occurrence of *rig pa* in every Buddhist text carries this exact technical meaning.

In this particular system, it does.

And the distinction matters because the goal is not to produce a permanently altered mental atmosphere.

It is to recognize the nature of awareness in whatever arises.

The nineteenth-century and later Tibetan commentarial tradition is emphatic on the danger of confusing stillness, clarity and bliss with realization.

Do-Ngak Chökyi Gyatso's Commentary on Some Terms from the Great Perfection explains rigpa as the direct recognition of the ground rather than simply another temporary cognitive state.

Now your teacher asks you to investigate.

A thought arises.

*I am meditating very successfully.*

You recognize the thought.

Another appears.

*This would make a magnificent book.*

Another.

*I wonder whether anyone else understands this as well as I do.*

Your teacher looks at you.

You become embarrassed.

And then you notice something.

The thoughts appeared without your consciously constructing them.

They remained briefly.

They dissolved.

And the knowing of their appearance wasn't itself something you could isolate as another independently existing object.

Now you make another mistake.

*Ah\! There must be a permanent witness behind the thoughts\!*

No.

Absolutely not.

This is where you could accidentally turn Dzogchen into a doctrine of an eternal Ātman.

The realization is not an independently existing observer that remains permanently identical while everything else changes.

That would be reintroducing the substantial self that Buddhist analysis has been dismantling since Chapter I.

Awareness is empty.

Its nature cannot be established as a fixed, self-existing entity.

The inseparability of awareness and emptiness is precisely what prevents the teaching from becoming an ordinary substance theory.

### Why isn't this just Kashmir Śaivism?

At this point your philosophical brain notices a comparison.

Trika Śaivism also gives extraordinary importance to the self-revealing nature of consciousness.

It discusses recognition.

It explains bondage through contraction and misapprehension.

And it presents liberation as the recognition of a reality that was never fundamentally absent.

The similarity is real and philosophically fascinating.

But the systems make importantly different metaphysical commitments.

Classical Pratyabhijñā Śaivism positively articulates the supreme selfhood of Śiva, his autonomous creative power and the manifestation of the universe through his freedom.

Dzogchen works within a Buddhist framework that rejects an intrinsically existent self.

Its language of primordial awareness should not automatically be equated with a metaphysically substantial universal subject.

Even sophisticated nondual comparisons must preserve this disagreement.

You can't simply declare\:

*Consciousness, emptiness, Śiva, rigpa, dharmakāya-same shit.*

That's how comparative philosophy becomes a spiritual smoothie in which every doctrine loses its distinctive meaning.

The more interesting question is how two systems can offer powerful accounts of recognition while giving different answers concerning selfhood, agency and the ontological status of consciousness.

But that is a subject for another book.

Because you have a more immediate problem.

You have now encountered rigpa.

Or, more precisely, within our fictional lineage narrative, you have received direct introduction and begun to recognize its meaning.

And the teacher tells you that the task is not finished.

You've recognized something.

Now you need confidence in that recognition.

And confidence that thoughts can liberate as they arise.

Which brings us to *trekchö*\.


## VI. Trekchö: Cut through the bullshit. Not the thoughts.

Tibetan:

Wylie: *khregs chod*\.

Often translated as *Cutting Through*, *Thorough Cut*, or *Cutting Through Solidity*\.

Now be careful.

The term doesn't mean taking a metaphysical sword and violently destroying every thought.

The characteristic Dzogchen approach is much subtler.

You recognize the nature of mind.

You gain confidence in that recognition.

And you learn to allow appearances to arise without reifying them into independent objects.

Your teacher points out that the mind is already producing thoughts.

It's extraordinarily good at it.

A remarkable thought factory.

Operating twenty-four hours a day.

No holidays.

No collective bargaining.

No quality control whatsoever.

And you've spent your entire life attempting to improve the output.

First you wanted pleasant thoughts.

Then sophisticated thoughts.

Then compassionate thoughts.

Then no thoughts.

Then extremely subtle thoughts about the absence of thought.

Now Dzogchen asks whether the basic problem was ever the mere occurrence of thought.

### The thought isn't the enemy.

Suppose anger arises.

In one approach, you might counteract it through patience.

A perfectly valid Buddhist method.

You could investigate its causes.

You could cultivate compassion toward the person who provoked it.

You could reflect upon the consequences of acting destructively.

In trekchö, the emphasis is on recognizing the nature of the arising itself.

Rather than following the anger into a story of self and enemy, its empty nature is recognized.

It need not be suppressed or elaborated.

The traditional metaphor is writing on water.

The letters appear.

But they do not remain as stable marks.

They dissolve as they arise.

Patrul Rinpoche uses precisely this image in the third section of his *Special Teaching of the Wise and Glorious King*, where he describes confidence in the liberation of arising thoughts.

Now let's make sure we don't misunderstand.

This is not the proposition that thoughts are irrelevant.

If you plan to harm someone, the fact that thoughts are empty does not mean you should simply proceed.

Conventional ethics still applies.

And beginners can very easily mistake an unexamined emotional reaction for something spontaneously liberated.

The tradition distinguishes genuine self-liberation from ordinary distraction and indulgence.

A thought genuinely self-liberated through recognition does not become fuel for further grasping.

But if anger develops into an elaborate personal grievance lasting six months, you haven't demonstrated spontaneous liberation.

You've demonstrated a remarkable capacity for holding grudges.

### Three ways of relating to thoughts

Now your teacher gives a useful comparison.

In ordinary distraction, a thought arises and you're captured by it.

*This person is insulting me.*

*I must defend myself.*

*I shall construct an elaborate rebuttal.*

The thought leads to further thoughts and emotional reinforcement.

In deliberate restraint, you recognize the anger as unwholesome and intentionally refrain from following it.

That's already valuable.

But in the Dzogchen account of self-liberation, the nature of the thought itself is recognized.

The arising does not need to be transformed into something else before its emptiness can be understood.

It is released without becoming the basis for another cycle of compulsive identification.

Here's the extraordinary implication.

**Liberation isn't necessarily about making the mind permanently empty of appearances. It concerns the cessation of ignorance in their apprehension.**

That is why the third statement of Garab Dorje's famous formulation concerns confidence in the liberation of what arises.

Not confidence that nothing will ever arise again.

Now consider death.

In Chapter II, you encountered the peaceful deities.

You were frightened.

The wrathful deities.

More frightened.

Lights.

Sounds.

Mandala forms.

An extraordinary visionary world.

And you treated the appearances as independently existing things confronting an endangered self.

You were trapped by your own interpretation.

But imagine having trained in the recognition of appearances without reification.

Then even an overwhelming experience need not be mistaken for an independent threat.

This is one of the fundamental connections between trekchö and the bardo of dharmatā.

The visionary display is not something you must conquer.

It is something whose nature may be recognized.

And when the nature is recognized, the ordinary mechanism of fear and attraction need not take hold.

Now you're getting somewhere.

You spend years stabilizing this recognition.

You practise in solitude.

You practise in daily life.

You encounter irritation, desire, fear and pleasure.

Some experiences are released immediately.

Others reveal how much attachment remains.

Sometimes you mistake a pleasant meditation state for realization.

Your teacher points it out.

Sometimes you become convinced you're progressing magnificently.

Your teacher asks whether you have become less self-centred.

Infuriatingly effective fellow.

But slowly, the practice becomes more stable.

And then, one day, you hear of another Dzogchen teaching.

A teaching concerned not primarily with resting in primordial purity, but with the visionary manifestations of spontaneous presence.

*Tögal*\.

And things are about to become gloriously weird.


## VII. Tögal: Apparently reality has a hidden light show, and the light show is not the fucking point.

Tibetan:

Wylie: *thod rgal*\.

Commonly translated as *Direct Crossing*, *Leaping Over*, or *Leap Over*\.

Tögal belongs particularly to the Dzogchen *Menngagde*, the Instruction Class, and its heart-essence traditions.

Its central visionary framework is associated with the spontaneous presence of luminosity.

Unlike the basic explanation of trekchö, tögal involves specialized practices and experiences relating to luminous appearances, often described through spheres of light, vajra chains, visionary mandalas and the manifestation of Buddha forms.

The practices are traditionally advanced and require appropriate instruction.

Some involve special conditions of light, darkness, posture and gaze.

We are not going to turn this into a do-it-yourself optical experiment.

In particular, **do not stare at the sun** or attempt prolonged light or darkness practices on the basis of a literary description.

They can pose real physical and psychological risks.

What matters for our inquiry is the metaphysics.

The tradition proposes that the luminous manifestations associated with the ground can become apparent in a structured series of visions.

And this is not merely ordinary visual entertainment.

The visions are interpreted within a process of realizing the empty, spontaneous nature of appearance.

Longchenpa's *Essential Summary* explicitly connects the sequence of direct perception, increasing experience, reaching full measure and exhaustion with the realization of the three kāyas.

Now let's explore them.

### Vision One: Direct perception of dharmatā

Tibetan: *chos nyid mngon sum*\.

The direct manifestation or perception of the nature of reality.

In traditional descriptions, luminous appearances arise.

Small spheres of light.

Patterns.

Subtle chains or linked forms of radiance.

The term *thig le* often refers to luminous spheres in these visionary contexts, although it has other meanings in tantric physiology.

The important claim is that these appearances are related to the intrinsic luminosity of awareness.

You, naturally, are overwhelmed.

After decades of meditation, you've begun encountering extraordinary visionary experiences.

Your first thought is\:

*Holy fucking shit.*

Your second\:

*I should write a book.*

Your third\:

*I wonder if anyone else has seen something like this.*

Your teacher has developed a special expression for moments like these.

It combines immense compassion with profound disappointment.

Because the real instruction is not to collect visions.

The point is recognition.

Luminous appearances are not automatically proof of realization.

Ordinary visual phenomena can arise through retinal processes, entoptic effects, sensory deprivation, neurological conditions and other mechanisms.

The presence of spots or colours doesn't establish that the Dzogchen visionary stages have been attained.

Within the tradition, the visions must be understood in relation to trained recognition, not mere optical novelty.

### Vision Two: Increasing experience

Tibetan: *nyams gong 'phel*\.

The visionary appearances develop.

They become more extensive.

More complex.

More structured.

The traditions describe an increasing richness of luminosity, with spheres, colours and patterns developing into elaborate configurations.

Now your teacher asks a very important question.

*What is increasing?*

You answer\:

*The visions.*

Yes.

But do the visions become intrinsically real merely because they're elaborate?

No.

And are they valuable because they give you an increasingly impressive collection of spiritual experiences?

No.

Their significance depends on the realization of their nature.

This is where the distinction between *nyams*, meditative experiences, and *rtogs pa*, realization, becomes important.

Experiences come and go.

They may be blissful.

Luminous.

Extraordinary.

Frightening.

And even spiritually meaningful.

But their presence doesn't automatically imply irreversible awakening.

You may see an elaborate mandala and still be attached to the identity of someone who sees elaborate mandalas.

**You can turn the entire visionary path into spiritual tourism if you aren't careful.**

The ego has acquired a new hobby.

Previously it wanted a nicer house.

Now it wants more luminous spheres.

Same grasping.

More interesting scenery.

### Vision Three: Awareness reaches full measure

Tibetan: *rig pa tshad phebs*\.

This is the stage traditionally described as the reaching of the full measure or maturation of awareness.

The visionary manifestations may become extraordinarily elaborate.

The Buddha families.

Mandala assemblies.

Forms of enlightened awareness.

Radiant patterns.

A vast symbolic articulation of the spontaneous display.

But do not imagine a simple linear progression in which every practitioner sees exactly the same images on the same dates.

The textual model presents a contemplative trajectory, not a standardized neurological test.

Now consider the philosophical significance.

In Chapter II, you encountered peaceful and wrathful deities after death.

You mistook them for independently existing threats.

Here, in the tögal tradition, related forms of luminosity and mandalic manifestation are approached within the context of trained recognition.

The relationship is not necessarily one of identical experiences.

The *Bardo Thödol* and the wider Dzogchen visionary traditions have related but distinct textual architectures.

Yet the underlying analogy is profound.

**What once appeared as an overwhelming cosmic danger can be encountered as a display whose empty nature is recognized.**

And at this level, the distinction between ordinary fear and wisdom becomes exceptionally sharp.

## XIII. The three kāyas: Buddhahood is not a little man becoming a gigantic cosmic man.

Now the final metaphysics.

The answer requires the doctrine of the three *kāyas*\.

The Sanskrit term *kāya* can mean body, collection or embodied dimension, depending on context.

In Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna Buddhism, the three kāyas articulate distinct but inseparable aspects of Buddhahood.

### Dharmakāya

The *dharma-body*\.

In Dzogchen, this is closely associated with primordial purity, the empty nature of awakened awareness, free from conceptual elaboration.

It is not a ghostly person made of invisible material.

It is not an immortal ego sitting inside an infinite space.

Nor is it simply a blank nothingness.

It concerns the ultimate dimension of Buddhahood.

The emptiness of intrinsic existence is inseparable from the wisdom of awakening.

### Saṃbhogakāya

Often rendered the *enjoyment-body*\.

This concerns the luminous manifestation of Buddhahood, traditionally represented through enlightened forms, mandalas and pure visionary appearances.

The Buddha families encountered in the bardo of dharmatā belong to this broad field of tantric interpretation.

Vairocana.

Akṣobhya.

Ratnasambhava.

Amitābha.

Amoghasiddhi.

The peaceful and wrathful mandalas.

Not simply physical people in exotic clothes.

Their luminous forms are part of the symbolic and metaphysical language through which awakened wisdom is represented.

### Nirmāṇakāya

The *emanation-body*\.

This concerns the manifestation of enlightened activity in forms accessible to beings.

The historical Buddha Śākyamuni is conventionally regarded as a nirmāṇakāya manifestation in many Mahāyāna presentations.

And, within broader tantric cosmology, enlightened activity can manifest in varied forms according to the capacities and needs of beings.

Here's the essential point.

**The three kāyas are not three independent Buddhas living in separate cosmic departments.**

They are inseparable dimensions of awakened reality.

Longchenpa's *Essential Summary* explicitly relates primordial purity to dharmakāya, spontaneous luminosity to saṃbhogakāya and responsive manifestation to nirmāṇakāya.

Now think about how this changes the meaning of death.

In ordinary saṃsāra, a sentient being experiences the dissolution of one body and the conditions for another birth.

The continuity is governed by karma and ignorance.

But in the case of a fully awakened Buddha, ordinary compulsory rebirth no longer applies.

Nevertheless, awakened activity is said to manifest for the benefit of beings.

That is the extraordinary difference.

A Buddha doesn't need to undergo involuntary rebirth to continue compassionate activity.

Manifestation is not propelled by ignorance in the way ordinary rebirth is.

Some Buddhist traditions distinguish the actual attainment of nirvāṇa without remainder from the broader Mahāyāna understanding of a Buddha's continuing enlightened activity.

And the language of the three kāyas offers one way of articulating how awakening can be free from saṃsāra while remaining compassionately efficacious.

The paradox begins to dissolve once you abandon the demand that every activity must belong to a metaphysically substantial person.

*So what makes them return?*

**The ignorant continuum is pushed by craving. The awakened Buddha is not.**

But we must be careful even here.

It would be a mistake to imagine an independent enlightened executive deciding which body to occupy next.

The doctrine is not a metaphysical version of booking an Airbnb.

## XVII. Why recognition actually liberates: The full causal mechanism.

We've spent five chapters using the word *recognition*\.

### Step one: A conditioned experience arises.

There is a perception.

A thought.

A sensation.

A luminous appearance.

Something appears.

The arising itself is not necessarily bondage.

### Step two: Its nature is misrecognized.

The appearance is apprehended as independently real.

The subject is apprehended as independently real.

A distinction which can function conventionally becomes reified into an apparently intrinsic division.

Here ignorance is operative.

### Step three: Grasping develops.

Because the subject and object appear intrinsically separate, the subject becomes concerned with acquiring, avoiding, defending and preserving.

Pleasure generates attachment.

Threat generates aversion.

Uncertainty generates anxiety.

Self-reference becomes increasingly elaborate.

### Step four: Intention produces karma.

Intentional actions arise through these afflictive structures.

The actions produce consequences.

They reinforce habits.

And, according to traditional Buddhism, they establish karmic potentials capable of ripening in future lives.

### Step five: The continuum remains conditioned by ignorance.

At death, the particular bodily basis ceases.

But according to the Buddhist rebirth doctrine, the causal continuity of consciousness is not exhausted.

Ignorance and karmic conditions support further becoming.

A new existence arises.

And the entire process continues.

The sequence is not a single simple mechanical chain, and Buddhist traditions differ over its detailed metaphysical interpretation.

But it expresses the basic structure of the saṃsāric problem.

Now ask\:

**What happens when the ignorance at the heart of the process is genuinely eradicated?**

Grasping no longer arises in the same deluded manner.

Afflictive karma is no longer generated through that fundamental error.

And the causal basis for involuntary rebirth is brought to an end.

This is the connection between realization and liberation.

Not that recognition emits a magical force that physically breaks the wheel of saṃsāra.

It undermines the ignorance through which the cycle is sustained.

Now, Buddhist traditions make further distinctions concerning residual karma, the stages of the path and the difference between initial realization and complete Buddhahood.

One genuine glimpse of emptiness is not necessarily the final exhaustion of every obscuration.

Our fifth-life story assumes that recognition has matured into complete liberation.

That is the decisive point.

### But why is this different from ordinary understanding?

**The prison isn't destroyed by acquiring better information about the prison. It's destroyed by ending the ignorance that makes the prison function.**

The highest Dzogchen account is not simply describing the relocation of an ordinary individual to another conditioned destination.

Have you become Samantabhadra?

The language of primordial awakening may describe the realization as inseparable from the nature Samantabhadra represents.

But it would be misleading to imagine that you have been physically absorbed into a gigantic cosmic Buddha.

Did your consciousness merge into an enormous universal ocean?

That too risks introducing a metaphysical substance theory foreign to the Buddhist rejection of inherent selfhood.

Then where are you?

Here is the philosophical trap.

**You're assuming that liberation must be another experience belonging to the same kind of individual that saṃsāra produced.**

But the teaching is precisely that the imagined independently existing individual was never ultimately established.

Now, does this mean nothing exists?

No.

Does it mean your ordinary life was meaningless?

No.

Does it mean the conventional person never lived, practised, loved or died?

No.

The conventional person existed dependently.

The actions mattered.

The compassion mattered.

The karma mattered.

And the path mattered.

What has been realized is the absence of the intrinsic selfhood previously attributed to those phenomena.

At the level of ultimate realization, it is not appropriate to imagine a little metaphysical person occupying a new location.

The question itself has smuggled the old assumption into the answer.

You are trying to locate a self whose intrinsic existence has been negated.

No wonder the tradition describes the fruition as beyond ordinary conceptual elaboration.

Now let's make one thing clear.

**This is not an experimentally established description of what happens to a person after death.**

It is the doctrinal account of complete liberation within the Dzogchen Buddhist system.

But within that system, it makes sense of the finality of awakening.

The causes of compulsory rebirth have ceased.

So the ordinary continuation driven by ignorance is no longer necessary.

And that brings us to an extraordinary complication.

Because our practitioner spent five lives cultivating compassion.

If liberation ends involuntary rebirth, what happens to the commitment to help other beings?

Does compassion simply stop?

Has the bodhisattva finally escaped the universe, leaving everybody else to sort out their own fucking problems?

Not according to the Mahāyāna understanding of Buddhahood.


## XIX. The Buddha returns. But this time nobody is being dragged back by karma.

Now recall the geckos.

Life Three.

You were feeding small animals, chanting mantras and wondering whether such actions could establish beneficial connections across rebirth.

You developed bodhicitta.

You aspired to complete awakening for the welfare of all beings.

And now, according to our fictional narrative, complete awakening has been attained.

Does that mean the commitment to compassion has become irrelevant?

No.

It is fulfilled through awakened activity.

In Mahāyāna Buddhism, a Buddha's manifestation for the welfare of beings is distinguished from the ordinary process of karmically compelled rebirth.

This is where the doctrine of the nirmāṇakāya becomes particularly important.

Enlightened activity can appear in forms appropriate to the needs of sentient beings.

As a teacher.

As a guide.

As a figure capable of conveying the Dharma.

And, in the more extraordinary scriptural accounts, in many other forms.

Remember Avalokiteśvara becoming a bee in the *Kāraṇḍavyūha Sūtra*?

There was a population of insects unable to receive conventional religious instruction.

The bodhisattva manifested in a form suited to them.

That story illustrates the extraordinary flexibility attributed to awakened compassion.

Now the distinction becomes clear.

The ordinary being is propelled through the cycle of rebirth by ignorance and karma.

The fully awakened Buddha is not.

But awakened activity can nevertheless manifest.

One is compulsory becoming.

The other is compassionate manifestation.

Now, are these descriptions metaphysically straightforward?

Absolutely not.

The relationship between Buddhahood, the three kāyas, the continuity of enlightened activity and the absence of inherent selfhood is one of the most sophisticated areas of Mahāyāna philosophy.

But the core distinction is intelligible.

**An awakened Buddha doesn't need to remain trapped in saṃsāra in order to benefit beings within it.**

This is not a person returning because their desire for existence has reappeared.

It is described as the spontaneous manifestation of awakened wisdom and compassion.

The *Prayer of Kuntuzangpo* explicitly presents the primordial Buddha's compassionate manifestations as appearing in varied ways according to the needs of beings.

Now imagine that, somewhere, a young practitioner is sitting beside a little animal.

They are uncertain whether their actions matter.

They want to reduce suffering.

They want to understand awareness.

They feel overwhelmed by the possibility of beginningless rebirth.

They are fascinated by Buddhist metaphysics but haven't yet fully investigated their own experience.

In the traditional Mahāyāna imagination, awakened activity might appear in whatever form is appropriate to guide such a being.

Perhaps through a teacher.

A text.

A conversation.

A condition that encourages compassion.

The tradition makes room for extraordinary manifestations, but it does not authorize us to identify every coincidental encounter as a supernatural intervention.

What matters is the principle.

Liberation and compassionate activity are not mutually exclusive.

And the highest fulfilment of the path isn't merely that your personal suffering has ended.

It's the realization of wisdom inseparable from the welfare of beings.

Which brings us to the final question.

**What actually distinguishes this outcome from the previous four deaths?**


## XX. The five deaths, finally understood.

Let's look back at the entire journey.

Not as an entertaining collection of Tibetan supernatural phenomena.

But as a coherent progression through the Buddhist theory of ignorance and liberation.

### Death One: Karma

You were an absolute bastard.

You harmed beings.

You cultivated hatred.

You died.

And within the traditional cosmology, the destructive karmic potentials matured into intensely painful rebirth.

The lesson wasn't that the universe contains a vindictive supernatural judge.

It was that intentional actions can condition the future of a sentient continuum.

**Karma matters because actions have consequences, including for the tendencies through which subsequent experience is apprehended.**

### Death Two: Recognition

You acquired intellectual understanding.

You understood the Buddhist vocabulary.

But your habits of fear, grasping and identification remained.

In the bardo, the peaceful and wrathful manifestations appeared.

You failed to recognize their nature.

You were attracted by familiar possibilities of conditioned existence.

And you entered another birth.

The lesson was that **knowing a doctrine is not identical to realizing what it describes**\.

### Death Three: Compassion

You cultivated merit.

You developed bodhicitta.

You learned mantras.

You aspired toward Buddha-fields.

You fed geckos who were utterly uninterested in your spiritual career.

Your actions were less dominated by self-centred grasping.

And your aspirations established a different relationship with the future.

The lesson was that **wholesome activity and compassionate intention can transform the causal orientation of a life**, although merit is not a mechanical guarantee of final liberation.

### Death Four: Meditation

You seriously investigated awareness.

You trained with dreams.

You studied the subtle body.

You developed familiarity with luminosity.

Your death was interpreted as a significant meditative event.

But subtle fixation remained.

You still wanted to be the person who had attained extraordinary realization.

The lesson was that **even remarkable meditative experiences do not automatically end ignorance**\.

### Death Five: Liberation

This time, the nature of mind was recognized without grasping.

The recognition matured.

The basic error of intrinsic selfhood was eradicated.

And with the exhaustion of ignorance, the causes of involuntary rebirth no longer sustained another conditioned existence.

The lesson was that **the ultimate problem was never merely which world you inhabited, but the ignorance through which experience was appropriated as an independently existing self confronting independently existing objects**\.


## XXI. A final source-critical reckoning: The Tibetans were not all describing exactly the same thing.

Before our last scene, there's one final scholarly distinction worth preserving.

Because the five chapters have drawn from several related but distinct traditions.

The *Bardo Thödol*, associated with Karma Lingpa's fourteenth-century treasure revelation, provides the celebrated instructions for the peaceful and wrathful appearances and the intermediate-state process.

The Geluk presentation of highest yoga tantra gives a particularly elaborate account of the eight dissolutions, subtle winds, the heart-centre clear light and completion-stage death yoga.

The Nyingma Dzogchen tradition, especially its heart-essence literature, develops the doctrines of primordial purity, spontaneous presence, trekchö and tögal.

And the broader Tibetan traditions preserve different forms of phowa, Pure Land aspiration and the interpretation of meditative states at death.

They are not interchangeable.

One especially valuable textual distinction emerged from the research for this chapter.

In Chapter II, we followed the classic **forty-two peaceful and fifty-eight wrathful** classification associated with the hundred peaceful and wrathful deities.

But the *Prayer of Kuntuzangpo*, transmitted through Rigdzin Gödem's Northern Treasures, speaks of forty-two peaceful Buddhas and **sixty blood-drinking Herukas**\.

That is a genuine difference in the enumerations used by the texts, not an invitation to silently change one number to fit the other. 

It is exactly the kind of detail that matters if we're trying to reconstruct the traditions faithfully rather than invent one homogeneous Tibetan afterlife manual.

Likewise, the bardo of dharmatā is described somewhat differently in different textual systems.

Mindroling Terchen's *Liberation Upon Hearing* provides an especially detailed account of luminosity, wisdom lights, the appearances of spontaneous presence and the relationship between meditation familiarity and the duration of bardo experience.

And Dodrupchen Jigme Tenpé Nyima's *Answers on Dzogchen* carefully distinguishes the recognition of ground luminosity at death from subsequent appearances in the bardo of dharmatā.

His text even addresses whether the peaceful and wrathful manifestations have different functions within the process of liberation.

That matters because a fully realized Dzogchen master need not pass through the same sequence of apparent opportunities as an ordinary confused being.

If liberation occurs at the primordial ground, the involuntary process is already interrupted.

The later bardo stages are not obligatory bureaucratic checkpoints which everyone must attend.

And rainbow body is not required for Buddhahood.

A practitioner might, according to the tradition, attain liberation without the corpse exhibiting any extraordinary behaviour whatsoever.

This is a crucial qualification.

**The absence of a miracle isn't evidence that someone failed to awaken.**

The deepest fruition is not evaluated simply by counting rainbows.


## XXII. The final word: What is death?

Now we're ready.

Five chapters.

Five lifetimes.

An entire cosmology.

Hell realms.

Hungry ghosts.

Peaceful Buddhas.

Wrathful Buddhas.

The bardo body.

Karmic judgment.

Womb-entry.

Merit.

Mantra.

Amitābha.

Sukhāvatī.

Tuṣita.

Death yoga.

Phowa.

Tukdam.

Bioelectricity.

The primordial ground.

Trekchö.

Tögal.

Rainbow body.

The three kāyas.

And now, after everything, the original question returns.

**What actually happens when you die?**

There are several different levels at which we must answer.

### Biologically

The present organism irreversibly ceases functioning as an integrated living human being.

The ordinary activity of the brain, body and nervous system ends.

The familiar capacities of sensation, thought, memory and action are no longer supported by that organism.

This is the domain of medicine and biology.

There is no established scientific evidence that personal consciousness survives irreversible death or travels into another organism.

### In the traditional Tibetan Buddhist account

The cessation of the gross body does not necessarily mean that the subtlest mental continuum has ceased.

The death process involves increasingly subtle dissolutions.

The clear light may manifest.

If the nature of mind is recognized, liberation may occur.

If it is not, the subsequent intermediate states may unfold.

The appearances encountered there provide further opportunities for recognition.

And if ignorance and karmic conditioning remain, another rebirth can occur.

This is the religious account, not an experimentally verified sequence.

### In the highest Dzogchen interpretation

The fundamental issue is more radical.

The nature of mind is primordially pure.

Appearances are empty and luminous.

Ignorance involves failing to recognize their nature and grasping them through the reification of subject and object.

The path introduces the practitioner to that nature and develops stable recognition.

The fruition is the exhaustion of ignorance.

And with that exhaustion, involuntary rebirth no longer follows.

Now comes the point which the title has been teasing.

**You're dead. Except there is no longer anyone to die.**

Strictly speaking, there was never an inherently existing person who could die.

But conventionally, there was a person.

That person lived.

Loved.

Practised.

Made mistakes.

Cultivated compassion.

And died.

Buddhism does not erase conventional existence merely because phenomena lack inherent existence.

The distinction is that liberation ends the delusion of intrinsic selfhood.

So the title is deliberately paradoxical.

The body dies.

The conventionally designated life ends.

But no independently existing ego has been destroyed.

Because no such ego was ever established.

And in the story's final Dzogchen realization, the causes through which another involuntary existence would arise have ceased.

That is the ending.

Not a little soul floating triumphantly into an infinite blue sky.

Not a permanent consciousness celebrating its escape from the material universe.

Not a cosmic person who has finally achieved the highest possible spiritual promotion.

Just the ending of the ignorance that sustained compulsive becoming.

Now there's one final question.

And it's the one that matters most.


## XXIII. You are alive.

Not another rebirth.

Not a sixth chapter.

**Right now.**

You're alive.

You can feel your body.

You can hear sounds.

You can notice thoughts.

You can experience pleasure.

You can experience fear.

And every one of those experiences is impermanent.

Something arises.

Something changes.

Something disappears.

And throughout the day, the mind repeatedly establishes a narrative.

*This is happening to me.*

*I want this.*

*I hate that.*

*I must become something.*

*I must avoid something.*

*I must preserve the person I imagine myself to be.*

The Tibetan tradition asks you to investigate that process.

Not merely to memorize what might happen after death.

Not merely to collect elaborate supernatural possibilities.

Not to become a scholar of the bardo while remaining completely ignorant of the attachments governing your present experience.

But to examine the mechanism now.

While you've still got a functioning body.

While you've still got access to teachers.

While you've still got the capacity to reflect.

While you've still got the opportunity to cultivate compassion and wisdom.

Because if the traditional account is true, what you repeatedly cultivate now may matter enormously when this life ends.

And even if its postmortem claims remain unverified, the questions it raises about identification, fear, impermanence and the nature of experience are available for serious investigation in this very life.

Now imagine the final scene of our five-death story.

A quiet monastery.

An old practitioner has died.

The body remains.

The companions carry out the appropriate rites.

There is no dramatic declaration of a miracle.

No celestial paperwork.

No final appearance of a cosmic judge.

No enormous sign in the sky announcing that the universe has finally been liberated from one particularly persistent idiot.

Outside, a small gecko emerges onto a wall.

It watches the room.

Its tiny body moves with extraordinary precision.

It pauses.

Looks around.

Then continues its hunt.

Nothing supernatural needs to happen.

Nobody has to claim that the gecko is an enlightened emanation.

Nobody needs to explain its appearance through karmic destiny.

It's simply a living creature going about its precarious little existence.

And somewhere in the world, another sentient being is asking the same questions that began our journey.

What am I?

What happens when I die?

Why do I suffer?

What continues?

How do I become free?

The Tibetan answer is not that these questions are stupid.

It's that they must eventually lead you beyond merely collecting answers.

Toward the direct investigation of the ignorance that generates suffering.

Toward compassion that no longer makes everything about you.

Toward wisdom that does not turn emptiness into another possession.

And, in Dzogchen, toward recognition of the nature that is not manufactured by practice but revealed through the ending of delusion.

The great masters of the tradition insist that this is not merely an intellectual conclusion.

A doctrine can be understood.

A book can be finished.

A philosophical argument can be won.

And yet the fundamental habits of grasping may remain entirely intact.

That is why practice matters.

You can spend your whole life studying maps of a territory without ever setting foot in it.

And you can spend five lifetimes learning about liberation while repeatedly reconstructing the conditions of bondage.

The invitation is to stop postponing the investigation.

Not to become obsessed with death.

But to understand life before death arrives.

Because your next moment of awareness is not guaranteed.

Your body is impermanent.

Everything you love will change.

And, according to the Buddhist analysis, suffering is perpetuated not merely by that change, but by the ignorance and grasping through which you demand that changing things provide an impossible permanence.

Now here is the final, beautifully inconvenient possibility.

Perhaps the most important thing you could learn from a book about death is not what extraordinary visions might appear after your heart stops.

Perhaps it is how completely you mistake the passing appearances of this very moment for an independently existing self and world.

And how different life might become if that mistake were understood.

No need to manufacture a cosmic spectacle.

No need to become a professional collector of mystical experiences.

No need to achieve the world's most impressive corpse.

Just a serious, sustained investigation into the nature of experience, inseparable from compassion for the beings sharing it.

**Five deaths.**

**Five lives.**

**An unimaginable amount of confusion.**

And finally, in the terms of the tradition, the possibility of recognizing what was never intrinsically bound.


**You are dead.**

For once, there is no next involuntary birth.

There is no sixth chapter.

There is no further attempt to become the person who escapes.

And the great joke is that the independently existing person you were trying to rescue was never there in the first place.

**The end of the five-death cycle.**
